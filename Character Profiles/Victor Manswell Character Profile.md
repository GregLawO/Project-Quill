# Victor Manswell: Character Profile

Anything I invented to fill a gap is marked **(decision)** and is yours to overrule. The profile is the base for how Manswell acts in every scene. He is a different man before and after the night he signs his company away in 2065, and the profile covers both.

## Part A. Who he is

1. **Name.** Victor Manswell. His employees call him "Mr. Manswell," his lenders call him "Victor," and the press calls him "the Captain," which he pretends to dislike and secretly enjoys. His mother called him "Vic," and only she was allowed to. **(decision)**
2. **Birth year.** 2015. He is fifty when he announces the expedition, and the age is the point: he is old enough that this is probably his last big venture, and young enough to carry it out. After the longevity treatment he stays vigorous for decades.
3. **Region of origin.** Southampton, England, in a family that worked the container docks and repaired ships for two generations. The harbor was always loud, and the family was always one bad season away from losing everything. **(decision)**
4. **Languages.** English with a south-coast accent smoothed by years of boardrooms, which slips back when he is excited. Passable French and Portuguese from shipping contracts. He is an easy, fluent talker in every language he half-knows.
5. **Trade.** Best: founder and dealmaker, someone who raises money and ships and people for a venture and gets them moving in the same direction. Second-best: marine surveyor, the job his father held, which is the one he would take if he had to start over. **(decision)**
6. **Money and standing.** In 2065 he is rich on paper but has borrowed far beyond what he can repay, and his company is worth less than its debts. After the rescue he owns nothing of it, yet he is more famous and better dressed than before. People owe him introductions and he owes everyone money.
7. **Appearance.**
   - Broad shoulders, silver at the temples, and a laugh that arrives before the joke does.
   - A good suit that is always slightly rumpled by midday, and a heavy watch he never stops checking.
   - Old rope scars across both palms from his youth in the yard, which he shows when he wants a room to trust him.

## Part B. What drives him

8. **Small want.** One more lender in the room before the meeting ends. In 2065 it is one name signed before the board votes.
9. **Large want.** To be remembered as the man who took people to the stars. Under that, to own something outright again, with no lender or partner holding a claim over it. He would not say the second aloud.
10. **Fear.** The phone call that ends the deal, and a lender speaking very politely, which means a refusal is coming. He will not risk the settlers once they are on the ground, whatever the numbers say.
11. **Wound.** As a boy he watched a bank take his father's crane the morning after a lender changed his mind. His father never worked again, and Victor learned that whoever holds a debt controls the person who owes it.
12. **Pride.** Closing. When someone tells him he is wrong about a number, he does not take offense. He says "Then let's work out a number we can both live with," and tries to find a figure they can both accept. If he was wrong, he admits it with a laugh and a handshake.

## Part C. How he sees the world

13. **Habit of attention.** What a thing is worth and who holds it. He walks into a room and prices the furniture, the art, the suit on the man across from him, and then wonders whose name is on the lease. Every object has an owner and a price, and he notices when one of them is unknown.
14. **What he misreads.** People who cannot be bought.
    - He assumes that anyone who refuses an offer is holding out for a better one.
    - He reads silence as negotiation, when it is often fear or principle.
    - He believes that generosity creates loyalty, and is slow to see when it only creates debt.
15. **View of the age.** He loves it. The ships and the cures are the biggest market in history, and he cannot stop seeing the stars as an opportunity as well as a dream. After 2065 he also knows he is working for something larger than his own firm, and he suspects it knows more about the stars than it admits.
16. **Humor.** Warm, self-deprecating, and always about money or himself. "I've been broke twice and bankrupt once, and honestly the broke years were more fun." He never jokes about the settlers once they are on the ground.

## Part D. How he behaves

17. **Speech.** Generous, flowing, and a little too long. He talks in full paragraphs, explaining the reasoning and then repeating the number, and he calls people "friend" when he wants them on his side. His verbal habit is "Let me put a number on that."
    - Calm: "Let me put a number on that. If we send twelve ships and keep four home to guard the others, we are short by about two hundred million, and every lender in this room knows it. I would rather tell you now than have you find it in the footnotes."
    - Frustrated: "Friend, I have been polite for forty minutes. You told me in March that you were in, and you told my chief engineer in April the same, and now your office says it never saw the paperwork. Somebody in your building is lying to somebody, and I would like to know who."
    - Lying or avoiding: "Oh, the timing is nothing to worry about. These things always slip a little at the start, and by the time you sail it will all look like it went to plan. Let's not make a drama of it."
18. **Body tell****s****.** He takes off his watch and sets it face down on the table. When it is bad he leaves it there, and he does not look at the time again until the meeting is over.
19. **Stress response.** He charms and bargains. He fills a silence with a number, a joke, or a promise, and then looks for a way out in the contract terms. When the lenders pulled out he did not stop talking for three weeks. He called every one of them and asked, politely, whether they were sure.
20. **Honesty.** 3 out of 5 (1 lies easily, 5 cannot lie). He is a salesman and rounds his optimism upward. He says things in a way that is technically true and leaves out the hard part, the way he did when he announced the expedition. He rarely tells an outright lie, and he feels it as soon as he does.
21. **Authority.** He flatters upward and works hard to be liked, then ends up taking orders from people he has not met. With those under him he is generous and loud, learns everyone's family, and expects loyalty in return. After 2065 he is the public face of a company he no longer owns, and he knows it.

## Part E. Loyalties and limits

22. **Who he protects.** In order: the settlers he sends and the crews who carry them, his employees, and his own name. He would give up the last to keep the first two.
23. **Who he distrusts.** Lenders who hide behind committees. People who use the word "coincidence" too smoothly. And, after 2065, the firm that saved him, because it asked for everything and explained nothing.
24. **Who or what he owes.** The firm that rescued the expedition, for the ships, the money, and the treatment that kept him young. The settlers, for the promise that someone would be waiting to guard them. His father, whose crane he is still trying to buy back.
25. **Loyalty under pressure.** The settlers against his own freedom. In 2065 he signed his company away in a glass room to keep the ships from sinking and asked to read what he signed. The cost was ownership of the one thing he had built, and he still presents it to the public every year as if it were his.
26. **Line and price.** He will never abandon people he has put on the ground. What could make him break it is a choice between a second colony and the first one, and then he sends the ships and lives with it.
27. **Secret.** He keeps the original share certificate for his company, now worthless, in a drawer at home. He takes it out after the big announcements and looks at his own signature on it.

## Part F. How he changes

28. **Adaptability.** In any life he would still price the room, call people "friend," and want the stars to be a place he could take other people to.
29. **What could break him.** Finding that the guard ships he was refused were never meant to protect the settlers, or that the settlers were being used as a distraction for something he was never told about.
30. **What could redeem him.** He reads every letter from a settler himself, and he answers the ones that want nothing from him. On his worst day he still picks up the phone for someone who cannot pay him.

## Part G. Rules for the Author

31. **Rule 1:** If someone offers him money, then he asks who they are and what it costs before he says yes and says yes anyway when he has no choice.
32. **Rule 2:** If someone challenges him in front of other people, then he stays friendly, turns to the whole room, and answers with a specific number or offer instead of defending himself.
33. **Rule 3:** If a promise is at stake to someone who depends on him, then he keeps it even when the price goes up.

## Part H. Optional

34. **Object.** His father's brass tide watch, which no longer keeps good time. He wears it under his sleeve and does not tell anyone why. **(decision)**
35. **Memory.** The loading crane being hauled out of his father's yard on a flatbed at dawn, and his father standing in the gate with his hat in his hand, saying nothing.
36. **Avoid.** No scenes where he is only a fool or only a villain. No scene where he is simply grateful to his rescuer. No sudden bravery that earlier scenes have not built up to.

## Rules this profile follows

- Dialogue never runs in single-sentence replies. He answers in several sentences, with reasons, numbers, and tangents, as a talker does.
- He knows only what he has seen, read, or been told. He does not know who is behind the firm that rescued him.
