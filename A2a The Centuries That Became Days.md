# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (31)*

### *Texas City, 2051*

I was seating the last gauge on the Med-Pod when Delgado came in through the back door of the shop with his dog in his arms.

The machine filled the bench in the back bay of his tire shop. It was a long steel cradle under a clear lid, with a tank behind it that I had made from a scrapped pressure vessel. The build diagram was pinned to the wall above it, with MED-POD written across the top. Almost all of the parts had come from a scrapper I built a several months ago, and my yard wages had paid for the rest.

This machine was exactly the same as the Med-Pod from the film Elysium, a bed that fixed anyone who lay in it. I knew exactly how it worked, down to every weld and every setting. What I did not know was how or why I knew it.

I woke in March of 2050 in a flat three streets from here, in a thirty-year-old body that was not mine. I think I used to be someone else, somewhere else, and I do not know how I came to be here. I had told no one.

A few months later, I built an automated scrapper and left it in a junkyard outside town, where it collected metal and parts. After that, I made a Phased Disassembler Array, a very crude one that took a long time to break scrap down into its base elements. From there, I built the tools that would make the tools for building the fantastic things I got floating inside my head. And now, I've rented a bay from Delgado and created the pod.

"A truck hit him on the ship channel road this morning," Delgado said. "The vet says the hip is broken in two places, and it's twenty-two hundred dollars to fix it, or she'll put him down. I don't have twenty-two hundred, and I can't do the other. I dunno why, but I thought maybe you'd look at him. You're good with things."

"Bring him over and keep the hip level," I said. "It takes ninety minutes, and you can stay."

I took Pancho from him, laid him in the cradle, closed the lid, and set the dials by hand. Delgado stood beside me with his cap in both hands, and neither of us spoke until the cycle ended.

When the lid came up, the dog got to his feet and shook himself from nose to tail. He went looking for a ball that wasn't there, and he ran the length of the bay on both back legs. Delgado made a sound I had never heard a grown man make. Then he sat down on an upturned tire and said "Okay" four or five times.

He asked me nothing. He did not ask what the machine was, how I had built it, or what he owed me. At the door he turned with the dog in his arms and said, "Thank you. I mean that. You're a good man." I looked at the gauge panel and told him to keep Pancho quiet for a week, and he carried the dog home with the cap pressed to his face.

I washed my hands twice and looked at the pod. It rebuilt a body from the inside, whatever shape the body was in. With the dials turned the other way, it made a person younger.

Every government, hospital chain, drug company, and criminal who heard of it would want it. Delgado had not asked what it was. The next person would, and whoever found out first would take the pod and the man who built it.

I took the diagram down, turned it over, and wrote a plan on the back. Step one was protectors, people who could guard me and each other against legal and illegal threats. Step two was a factory.

I had one thing to pay with, and it was lying in the cradle. Anyone dying or broken could come out whole. I would look for people willing to give me their lives in exchange for the ones I gave back. A life for a life. During the treatment I would add a failsafe that made sure they stayed loyal.

Under the plan I drew two columns. The left said PRIMITIVE and the right said LEVEL 2. Level 2 was the pod on my bench. It healed a dying person completely, made them young again, and slowed their aging for half a lifetime. I would keep it for myself.

Primitive would be a cruder machine that cured disease and injury. That was the one I would share with the world, so it had to be something the world could have built without me. I would not build it myself. I pinned the sheet back on the wall with the plan facing out.

The first name on my list was Dominic Vance. A volunteer at the veterans' hall in Galveston had given it to me in the spring: a retired Marine gunnery sergeant who had trained half the reservists in the county. He was dying of ruined lungs in a hospice in Houston.

On the first Monday of July I stood in the corridor outside his room, with a rented van and a gurney waiting at the curb. In a minute I would ask a dying man to trust a stranger with what was left of his life.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Helen Marchetti (52)*

### *Houston, 2051*

**October 2051.** The man at the foot of her bed had brought no paper. He held no folder, no phone, and no pen. In twenty-eight years of negotiations, Helen Marchetti had never sat across from anyone with nothing in their hands. The visitor and his men had just brushed past the nurses' desk without signing in and nobody had even looked up to question that.

His men stood in the doorway. One was broad, in an overcoat too warm for the ward, and he watched the corridor instead of her. The other carried a black case the size of a toolbox and watched the window.

Samuel Ortega sat in a wheelchair beside the bed with an oxygen line over his ears. He had come at four every afternoon for three weeks. His heart had been failing since spring and her kidneys since summer.

"My name is Kenji Himura," the man said. "I can heal both of you. I'll tell you what I want in return, and you can stop me whenever you like."

"Before that," Helen said, "who are your friends? A man who needs two guards at a hospital door has been threatened by someone, and I'd like to know who."

"Dominic Vance and Teodor Ashby. They keep me safe, and they'll stay at the door. The threat is from people who would want what I have." He said it flatly. "I have a machine that can rebuild a body into its healthiest version. Not just your kidneys, Ms. Marchetti, or your heart, Mr. Ortega. My machine can even bring you back your prime years."

Ortega laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough. "Helen, a man in a parking lot told me the same thing in August. He wanted six thousand dollars."

"I don't want money," Himura said. "I want your expertise and your loyalty."

Helen capped her pen and uncapped it. "How did you find us?"

"You both went on medical leave from the same firm in the same month. Your firm builds charitable foundations, and I need one built. I looked for the best people I could find, and the best ones were also running out of time."

"It sounds like you read our docket," she said. "What do you want built?"

"A few foundations in different countries. Foundations that will own the things I will design. If someone sues me, they have to go through these foundations, and they would be run by people who read every page."

"Who owns these foundation, and who signs for them?"

"No one owns them. They'll own the designs, and a trustee signs for them. I haven't chosen the trustees. I'd like them to carry a name that isn't mine."

"What name?"

He took a moment. "Tallis Bay." He said it again, more quietly, to himself.

She wrote it on the legal pad without looking up. She knew no company, street, or family by that name.

"There's one more thing, and I'm telling you before you sign, not after," he said. "When you're well, there's a session that teaches you some of what you'll need to know. It also puts something in your head that stops you from telling anyone outside the organization what we do or who runs it. You'll still know it, but you won't be able to say it, and nothing on Earth will be able to change that."

Helen put both hands flat on the blanket. "That's a clause I can't read, can't amend, and can't waive."

"Yes. I can't change that part, and I won't pretend I will."

"People usually bury those on page nine. I'd like to know why you didn't."

"I'd rather you heard it from me than found it there."

Ortega had been watching her instead of Himura. "Helen, my daughter turns nine in March. I'd like to see her grow into a woman. Sign the damn thing."

"I will," she said, "but I'm going to write down that I was told first." She tore a page from the pad, wrote it out, dated it, and held it out. He read it twice before he initialed it.

**December 2051.** The foundation papers were due at the state office on the thirty-first. If they missed that date, the first gift could not be accepted tax-free this year. On the twentieth Helen was still at her desk at eight in the evening, six weeks off dialysis, reading the last schedule aloud to herself.

Himura arrived at nine with the same two men, who took the same places by the door. He read the contract standing up and stopped at the last clause.

"This says the treatments continue for as long as someone serves. That's the clause I wanted, but didn't know how to write."

"One question. What happens to everyone's term if you die?"

He took a moment. "I haven't had to answer that yet."

"Then answer it now. Someone has to hold every term and every treatment after you, and I can't file a foundation that doesn't say who."

"Tallis Bay holds them. I'd like you to be its trustee."

She set the pen down. "You know what a trustee can do. If you wanted something and I thought it was wrong, I could refuse you, and the foundation would stop with me."

"I know."

"Then why me?"

"Because you asked what happens if I die before you asked about the money. And because you'll read a page to the end before you stop me."

She uncapped the pen and wrote *trustee* in the margin beside her own name.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Byron Pyrrus (68)*

### *Toledo, July 2052*

The drawings in the young man's folder held tolerances that Byron Pyrrus's toolroom would need three weeks to hit. He saw it from the hospital bed in the first minute, before he had read a single note. He turned the top sheet toward the window and found the lid housing. It would have to be cast in one piece, and only his Cleveland plant had a press big enough.

"Cleveland can do the lid," he said. "Nobody else can. Who drew this?"

"I did," the young man, Kenji Himura, answered. He sat in the visitor's chair with his back to the wall and a second folder on his knee.

"You've never run a line."

"No, I haven't, and that's why I'm here. I can draw a part, but I can't tell what a plant can cast."

Byron turned his steel ring around his finger. Through the half-open door he could see four men standing in the corridor, not talking and not moving. None of them carried a tool bag.

"All right," he said. "Tell me what it is."

"It's a pod that cures disease and injury: cancer, infection, broken bones. I want the world to be able to believe somebody could have built it without me."

"Can it cure this?" Byron lifted two fingers toward the oxygen line at his nose.

"This version can't. Yours has gone too far, and it would clear the cancer without giving you your lungs back. I have another machine in Houston that can, and more. I'd move you tomorrow, and you'd be well by Monday morning."

"My board meets Monday."

"Then you'd be in the room." Himura answered at once. "In return, Oyama Industries will belong to me, and will build what I design under the name Tallis Bay. You'll remain the chairman."

Byron had run Oyama for forty years and had no one to hand it to. Nine thousand people drew pay from it. "What happens to my workers if you disappear?"

"The contract says that if Tallis Bay ever stops paying, every design it holds becomes Oyama's to build and sell. A lawyer named Marchetti wrote that clause, and she'll go through it with you on Monday."

"Nobody has ever protected my payroll before I asked."

"Then what do you need besides that?"

"Time." Byron thought it through. "I can have ten of these pods on the floor in a month if I pull Cleveland off the work it's doing. After that, fifty a month. What do you want by Friday?"

"Nothing by Friday. Ten in a month is enough to start."

Byron pulled the second folder toward him. The sheets showed a figure of a man's height, drawn from the front, side, and back, with hinged joints at the knee, hip, and shoulder and a thick plate over the chest. "This is a robot."

"It's a security droid. It walks a fence all night and doesn't tire. It's also not for sale, to anyone, ever. Oyama builds it for the foundation, and you keep the design where no one else can find it."

"I'd sell a thousand of these."

"You wouldn't have a customer who didn't want to know whose design it was. The first buyer who learned that would try to take it, and he'd start with you."

Byron looked at the chest plate. A square cavity was drawn behind it with no part number. "What goes here?"

"A data crystal. I'll bring those myself, and the brains that run on them. You build the body."

"So you want me to build you a man out of steel, and you'll supply the rest."

"Yes."

Byron laughed, and the laugh set him coughing. When it stopped, he took a pen from the tray and read the contract to the last page. It took him eleven minutes.

"I'm already sixty-eight," he said. 

"You'll have more than sixty-eight years."

He signed. "Don't thank me. Get me to Houston. I want the Cleveland press running on that lid by Friday, and I'm going to order it from the bed." He slid the second folder back across the blanket. "The droid drawings go in my own safe, not the plant's. Safes at the plant get opened by people who have keys. Nobody else has the key to mine."

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (32)*

### *Houston, October 2052*

The newest man in the warehouse stood with his palm flat against a roof post. I had met him that morning. Danny Shepard was a combat engineer from Galveston whose legs had been crushed in a building collapse five years earlier, and the pod had given them back in September. His left foot still trailed a little on the stairs.

"Something wrong with that post?" I asked.

"That's load-bearing, and it's carrying more than it was built for." He did not take his hand away. "There's a crack at the top joint. You feel it better than you see it. It'll hold until somebody puts a ladder against it, and then it won't, and I'd rather not find that out with a man on the ladder."

"Can you fix it, and how long will it take?"

"A brace and two days. I can start tonight, sir, if you want it done."

"I do. What do you need?"

"Steel plate and bolts, and it's all in the truck."

I left him to it. The warehouse was a rented shell east of the city with a concrete floor, one drywall office, and a roof that leaked in two places. Marchetti and Ortega were in the office with the door shut, writing the last of the contracts.

At a trestle table by the wall, three people who had worked for intelligence services were reading court dockets to find the people who would one day buy what I made. Ashby and another radio engineer were stringing aerial wire across the rafters. Every minute or so Ashby stopped and tilted his head at the sound the wire made.

Six more fighters stood in the open middle of the floor in the first frames Oyama had built for me. A frame was a steel skeleton that took the weight of the man inside it and everything he carried, and it ran on nothing but his own legs. The next version needed a small reactor. I could draw the reactor, but I could not pay for the metal.

Along the east wall stood four security droids. They had come from Byron's Cleveland plant on Tuesday, built with steel he had paid for himself. Each was a little over six feet of gray plate on a steel frame, with one of my data crystals in its chest. A droid was for the hours of the night when a man cannot stay awake, and when one of them took a step you could hear it across the whole floor.

Vance wanted me to see one work. He stood a training dummy upright in the open floor, a man-sized torso with jointed arms on a weighted base, and told the nearest droid to engage it.

The droid crossed the floor. Every step rang off the concrete.

It shifted its weight onto its back foot.

A gray hand took the dummy's wrist. Plate clanged. The dummy went over.

A knee settled on its chest. The other hand stopped an inch from its throat.

I put my palm flat on the trestle table.

Then the droid let go and stepped back to the wall. Nobody on the floor spoke.

I knew that move. A man shifts his weight onto his back foot, takes the wrist with the near hand, and drops the opponent as his balance goes. I had watched Vance do it to that same dummy in September.

Fifteen months earlier I had walked into a hospice room and told a dying man what I wanted. He said he had nothing to pay with but what was in his head, and I said that was what I wanted. I put him in the pod, and while he was in it I copied out everything he knew: the training, every lesson from every place he had been shot at, the way he stood in a doorway.

When he came out he asked what he was supposed to do, and I told him to teach people to guard me. He said, "Walk the ground first." We still start every job that way. Every move those droids knew had come from him.

Late that night, when the others had gone, I went to the back of the warehouse, where two frames lay on a bench. They had no skin and no faces yet. Their joints were finer than the droids', and their hands could one day pick a lock. I stood over them and asked what they were for.

They would guard me, and they would look like men. I was building them myself with money from Oyama, and they would take three years. Until then Vance and Ashby would walk with me.

I took my notebook from my coat and wrote *2055* at the top of a clean page. Under it I wrote three lines: *platoon full*, *droid squads in place*, *a guard at every site*. I would sell nothing to anyone until all three were true.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Eleanor Whitcombe (60)*

### *Houston, February 2053*

The letter in Senator Eleanor Whitcombe's handbag gave the name of her cardiologist, the date of her last scan, and one sentence about how long the doctor had said she had. She had told no one alive those three things. Helen Marchetti, a lawyer she had known for twenty years, had handed it over in an envelope she had not opened. Eleanor had watched her face while she did, and Helen had looked at the envelope the whole time.

She had come to a rented warehouse east of the city on a wet Tuesday, with her driver waiting at the end of the lot and her committee due to vote on Thursday. She had spent twenty years watching who looked at whom before anyone spoke. In this room everyone looked at Kenji Himura. He looked at no one but her.

The older man by the door, in a gray coat, watched the entrance. The younger one, with the radio case, watched the older man. Marchetti stood a little behind Himura's shoulder, looking at the floor.

"Mr. Himura," Eleanor said. She kept one hand on the back of a folding chair. "You have my cardiologist's name in an envelope. In my line of work that's called a threat."

"I didn't mean it as one." He said it at once, and he did not look away. "My people found your records. I should have asked you first. I didn't, because I didn't think you'd come if I did. I'm sorry."

"Then why put it in the letter?"

"So you'd know I could help. I can heal your heart completely, not manage it for a few more years." He pulled a second folding chair away from the wall and sat down on it. Eleanor sat as well.

"That's generous," she said. "Before I ask what you want, who spoke first about me, you or Helen?"

"I did. My staff found the records, and I asked Ms. Marchetti to carry the invitation."

"She did." Eleanor pushed her reading glasses up onto her head. "What do you want?"

"I want you to lead people. I'll have clinics in Galveston, Marseille, and Singapore by summer. They'll offer organs grown from a patient's own cells, and the paperwork has to be real, with a license no minister can pull on a whim. I need someone who knows who to call in every ministry, and who can build a group of people who would rather ask questions than close a clinic. People in your line of work call them factions."

"They are. And in return?"

"Health now, and a long life. Youth later. If a senator looks thirty next year, people will ask why, so I'd wait ten years, until no one will. Anyone you bring in on the same terms gets the same."

"Terms."

"Service for life. A life given for a life. There's a session after the treatment, and part of it stops you from telling anyone outside what we do. Helen can read you the exact wording."

"I'd rather read it myself." Eleanor looked at the older man by the door, and then at Himura again. "Am I free to leave?"

"Yes, right now. Vance will hold the door."

"I'm sure he's good at it." She almost laughed. "I vote on Thursday, as it happens, on the device licensing bill. I came here meaning to ask what you'd want from that."

"Vote however you were going to. I'm not asking you to change anything. What I want comes later, when someone tries to close one of my clinics, and I want one person in the room who can say wait. What do you need from me before you decide?"

"A day," she said, and then, "No. A day wouldn't tell me anything new." She stood and pressed two fingers against her father's gold pin. "Yes. I'll lead them. Say the cities again."

"Galveston, Marseille, Singapore."

She wrote the three names on the back of her committee agenda with the pen from her jacket.
