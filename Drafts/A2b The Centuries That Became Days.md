# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (23 and 25)*

### *Galveston, 2054 and 2056*

**November 2054.** The man across the table finished everything on his plate, down to the parsley. Hannah watched him do it, because nobody ate like that unless they had once gone without. Her mother had set his place beside her father, and he sat with his back to the wall and the kitchen door in the corner of his eye, in a house where nobody was going to attack him over pot roast.

She was home on leave in her dress blues, and this was the first time she had seen Kenji. Her father was cheerful, and her mother was watching everyone, which was her way. Hannah had refused the clinic’s upload a year ago, and she had been polite and cold ever since. Nobody at the table mentioned it.

He called her father “sir” and her mother “ma’am,” and he never once used their first names. No one had come with him, and no one was mentioned: no wife, no brother, no town he missed. Hannah noticed those gaps the way she noticed a missing name tag, and she filed them in the same place.

“What do you do when you’re not at the clinic?” she asked.

“I build things,” he said. “Mostly in the evenings, in a rented bay with one good lathe. Small things that other things get made with.”

“What kind of things?”

“Tools, mostly. Gauges and jigs and a lot of drilled holes. It’s less interesting than it sounds, and I’d rather be useful than interesting.”

She could hear that it was true. She could also hear how little it held.

When the plates were empty he cleared them without being asked and squared the stack on the counter. Hannah went up to bed not liking him. But she had caught him in nothing, and that bothered her more than a lie would have.

**March 2056.** The wall behind the front desk was still bare paint, and Hannah had stopped expecting a license to appear on it. She had left the Navy in February, because her father had talked her into the security post and because the place bothered her too much to leave to someone else. Three weeks in, she walked the building with Kenji on a Thursday and read him what was missing. She carried her father’s tape measure in her jacket pocket, and she had already measured every door herself, because she trusted her own numbers more than the floor plans.

“There is no license on the wall, and nobody wears a name tag,” she said. “The east wing has a locked door that I’m not allowed past. Two grey frames the size of men stand in the back corridor, and my father calls them equipment. The door crew is better trained than a clinic would pay for. Walk me through any of it, starting with the frames.”

“I can’t answer that,” Kenji said. “I’d rather tell you so than make something up.”

She asked about the locked wing and got the same words. She asked about the license and the name tags and got them again, each time with his eyes on her face. Then she asked whether she could retrain the door crew her way, and he said yes without a pause, and by Friday it was done.

“Which of these can you explain?” she asked on the way back to the desk.

“None of them, to you,” he said. “Not yet, and I’m sorry that’s the honest answer.”

“Yet,” she said. “That word has a promise in it, and I’ll hold you to it.”

That evening she took a sheet of paper from the drawer, folded it in thirds, and wrote the first line. *Why does he stand at the edge of every room?* It was a question she had decided not to ask him.

**June 2056.** The hospital chain’s men came on a Tuesday morning with a letter on good paper and a tool bag. The letter said inspection. The bag said something else. Hannah’s father was already in the back corridor, under the pipe that ran above the east wing door, working at a valve that had dripped since April. Two men she had not hired stood by the door and would not look at her, and at the far end Kenji had one hand on the door frame and did nothing, and she took it for fear. The two grey frames stood along the wall beside the wing door, as they always did.

Hannah stepped between the men and the wing door.

“You’re leaving,” she said. “Now, and quietly.”

The nearer man opened the bag.

A steel pipe, taped at one end.

Her fist locked around the tape measure in her pocket.

“Darling,” her father said, very softly.

His hand went to his back pocket.

At the far end Kenji had not moved. His hand stayed on the door frame.

The pipe came up.

Behind her, servos shrieked.

Another hand closed around the hand that held the pipe.

Metal.

The pipe dropped. A second metal hand caught it before it hit the floor.

The other man lunged in that same second. Joints clanged. He hit the floor face first, and a knee settled between his shoulder blades.

Cable ties snicked, one, two.

Two grey shapes stood over the men.

Robots.
