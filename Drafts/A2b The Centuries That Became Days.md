# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (23 and 25)*

### *Galveston, 2054 and 2056*

**November 2054.** The man across the table finished everything on his plate, down to the parsley. Hannah watched him do it, because nobody ate like that unless they had once gone without. Her mother had set his place beside her father, and he sat with his back to the wall and the kitchen door in the corner of his eye, in a house where nobody was going to attack him over pot roast.

A fifth place stood at the end of the table, the plate turned upside down and a napkin folded beside it. Her brother Danny was in Houston on a security contract and had been home twice in five years, and her mother set his place every Sunday anyway. The new man looked at the empty chair once, for half a second longer than a stranger would, and Hannah noticed that he did not ask whose it was.

She was home on leave in her dress blues, and this was the first time she had seen Kenji. Her father was cheerful, and her mother was watching everyone, which was her way. Hannah had refused the clinic’s upload a year ago, and she had been polite and cold ever since. Nobody at the table mentioned it.

He called her father “sir” and her mother “ma’am,” and he never once used their first names. No one had come with him, and no one was mentioned: no wife, no brother, no town he missed. Hannah noticed those gaps the way she noticed a missing name tag, and she filed them in the same place.

“What do you do when you’re not at the clinic?” she asked.

“I build things,” he said. “Mostly in the evenings, in a rented bay with one good lathe. Small things that other things get made with.”

“What kind of things?”

“Tools, mostly. Gauges and jigs and a lot of drilled holes. It’s less interesting than it sounds, and I’d rather be useful than interesting.”

She could hear that it was true. She could also hear how little it held.

When the plates were empty he cleared them without being asked and squared the stack on the counter. Hannah went up to bed not liking him. But she had caught him in nothing, and that bothered her more than a lie would have.

**March 2056.** The wall behind the front desk was still bare paint, and Hannah had stopped expecting a license to appear on it. She had left the Navy in February, because her father had talked her into the security post and because the place bothered her too much to leave to someone else. Three weeks in, she walked the building with Kenji on a Thursday and read him what was missing. She carried her father’s tape measure in her jacket pocket, and she had already measured every door herself, because she trusted her own numbers more than the floor plans.

“There is no license on the wall, and nobody wears a name tag,” she said. “The east wing has a locked door that I’m not allowed past. Two grey frames the size of men stand in the back corridor, and my father calls them equipment. The door crew is better trained than a clinic would pay for. Walk me through any of it, starting with the frames.”

“I can’t answer that,” Kenji said. “I’d rather tell you so than make something up.”

She asked about the locked wing and got the same words. She asked about the license and the name tags and got them again, each time with his eyes on her face. Then she asked whether she could retrain the door crew her way, and he said yes without a pause, and by Friday it was done.

“Which of these can you explain?” she asked on the way back to the desk.

“None of them, to you,” he said. “Not yet, and I’m sorry that’s the honest answer.”

“Yet,” she said. “That word has a promise in it, and I’ll hold you to it.”

That evening she took a sheet of paper from the drawer, folded it in thirds, and wrote the first line. *Why does he stand at the edge of every room?* It was a question she had decided not to ask him.

**June 2056.** The hospital chain’s men came on a Tuesday morning with a letter on good paper and a tool bag. The letter said inspection. The bag said something else. Hannah’s father was already in the back corridor, under the pipe that ran above the east wing door, working at a valve that had dripped since April. Two men she had not hired stood by the door and would not look at her, and at the far end Kenji had one hand on the door frame and did nothing, and she took it for fear. The two grey frames stood along the wall beside the wing door, as they always did.

Hannah stepped between the men and the wing door.

“You’re leaving,” she said. “Now, and quietly.”

The nearer man opened the bag.

A steel pipe, taped at one end.

Her fist locked around the tape measure in her pocket.

“Darling,” her father said, very softly.

His hand went to his back pocket.

At the far end Kenji had not moved. His hand stayed on the door frame.

The pipe came up.

Behind her, servos shrieked.

Another hand closed around the hand that held the pipe.

Metal.

The pipe dropped. A second metal hand caught it before it hit the floor.

The other man lunged in that same second. Joints clanged. He hit the floor face first, and a knee settled between his shoulder blades.

Cable ties snicked, one, two.

Two grey shapes stood over the men.

Robots.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (37)*

### *Galveston, 2057*

**April 2057.** Hannah measured the lid of the cooler with her father’s tape before she would sign for it. The cooler had no maker’s plate, no serial number, and no stamp on the hinges, so she wrote its length on the custody sheet in pencil. Kenji watched from the monitor room doorway, where the wall was at his back and the clock was in plain view. It was twenty past nine, and Dr. Okonkwo stood on the other side of the front desk with her coat still buttoned and her eyes on that clock.

The surgeon had driven forty minutes from the county hospital with one patient on her mind and one rule in her pocket. The registry’s rule was old and simple: a patient whose operation was not logged by midnight on the day his kidney was ready lost his place to the next name and went back to waiting. Two years ago her list had run to two pages. The clinics had grown skin for burn wards, then livers, then whole kidneys from a patient’s own cells, and the pages had emptied until only this man was left.

Kenji did not look at the cooler. He looked at what it was for. Tonight it was for one man, but by next month every hospital between here and Houston would want one, and the pods he could build in a year would fill a county’s lists and not a state’s. He had promised himself in a tire shop that the spread would wait until the people behind it were ready, and last June’s shakedown had shown him what the waiting was for. Hannah at the desk and Tom in the corridor stood in front of whoever came for this. He had known that since he built the first pod, but it was another thing to see it arrive.

“Walk me through where it’s been,” Hannah said, with the pencil above the sheet. “There’s a line here for the donor, and I’d like to know what to write on it. I don’t mind a strange answer. I mind a blank.”

“There is no donor,” Kenji said. “It was grown from his own cells in this building, and it hasn’t left that cooler since nine o’clock. You can write that on the line.”

Hannah held the pencil still. Then she wrote *none* and asked nothing more, though the next question was plain in her face. Kenji was relieved, but he disliked being relieved.

Dr. Okonkwo lifted the tag from the handle and read it. It carried the clinic’s licensed name, a batch number, and the state’s approval stamp, and the line for a maker was blank. The clinic’s license lay in a drawer under a shell company’s name, but the stamp was real. She slid the tag into her coat pocket and did not ask. Nobody who took anything from him ever asked, because when a person needs a thing badly enough and the paperwork is in order, the only question left is whether it works.

Hannah signed at nine forty-one, and the cooler was out the door with hours to spare.

**Twenty to one.** The call had come at midnight, and the patient would live. Kenji sat at the steel table in the staff kitchen with his back to the wall, and Hannah set a plate of cold eggs in front of him without a word. He ate all of it, down to the last corner of toast, and when the plate was clean she was watching him with her chin on her fist.

“You finish everything,” she said. “I saw it at my mother’s table the first night, and I’ve watched you do it ever since. Nobody does that unless they’ve gone without.”

“I didn’t always have enough,” he said.

She nodded once and let it be. “Last June, when the men came with the pipe, you stood at the far end of the corridor with your hand on the door frame, and I took it for fear. Walk me through what you were doing.”

“Those two machines are the clinic’s guards,” Kenji said. “They moved the moment the pipe came up, because that is what they are for, and there was nothing left for me to do. I stood still so I wouldn’t be in their way. I should have told you last March, when you asked me to my face. You run this building’s security, and I let your father call them equipment while you planned doors around two things you didn’t know could move.”

“Where did they come from?”

“I can’t answer that, and I won’t invent something. I’m sorry I never told you what they were.”

“I thought you were afraid,” Hannah said.

“I wasn’t afraid of the men. I was watching to see that you were clear of them, and you weren’t, because you had walked toward them. If I had told you the rest, you would have known you didn’t need to.”

She was quiet for a while. Then she turned the empty plate a quarter turn on the steel. “I’ll tell you something back, so it’s even. I took this job because my father asked, and that’s the reason I gave. The reason underneath was you. I wanted to know what you were hiding. Tonight you told me something true about this building without my asking twice, so I’m staying.”

Kenji squared his fork against the edge of the table.

“Is there more coffee?” she said.

He got up and found the pot. He had believed she took the post for her father’s sake. She had taken it to look at him, and she had been looking ever since. Everyone else who came through the clinic looked at what it did, and he found he would least like to lose the one who looked at him.
