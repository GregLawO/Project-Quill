# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (33)*

### *Galveston, 2053*

**June 2053.** The name on the referral list was T. SHEPARD, pancreas, advanced, and Danny Shepard had stood in the doorway of the monitor room for a full minute without telling me why he was there. The clinic had opened two weeks earlier. Twelve screens on the wall showed the public floor, the hidden wing, the dock, and the street.

"Come in," I said. "What do you need?"

"My father's on your list." He put his palm flat against the doorframe. "Tom Shepard. He took me in when I was eight, after my real dad's cable snapped and dropped him off a gantry. He's got three weeks, maybe. I've never asked you for anything."

"I know."

"I'm asking."

I had a rule against this, made in January. If I saved a man's father, he could never leave me without choosing between me and his family, and I did not want anyone serving me because I had made it impossible to stop. But I looked at Danny's hand on the frame.

"The public floor runs a Primitive pod," I said. "It would clear the cancer, but he's too far gone for it to hold him together afterward. He needs the Level 2 wing. I'll move him there tonight."

He let out a breath he had probably held since he read the list. "Thank you, sir. One more thing. I don't want my family knowing where I work."

"I can't promise they won't ask you. I can only promise I'll never tell you to lie to them."

"You don't have to. The directive stops me halfway through the sentence, and I change the subject. My mother says I always did."

Three days later, at ten past six in the morning, a young woman sat in the waiting room with a folded uniform on the chair beside her, where she had sat for most of those three days. I watched her on the fourth screen from the left. The staff had been told to say nothing, so she had spent three days looking at the bare square of wall behind the front desk.

I had taken the license down on the first morning we opened, because it named Tallis Bay and its trustee, and a trustee has a home address. I had thought about the empty wall for maybe four seconds, and I had been wrong. Hannah Shepard had spotted it in the first hour.

Danny stood behind my chair. He had come in without a word and laid his palm flat against the wall, and neither of us spoke while the frosted doors opened and Tom walked out on his own feet.

"He's standing straight," Danny said, very quietly. "Look at him. He's standing straight."

On the screen, Tom looked at the bare wall, then at his daughter, and said something that made her cross the room and take his wrist. Danny took his hand off the wall.

"Side door," I said. "Now, before she looks up."

"Yes, sir."

The card was in my pocket. At four that morning I had sat with a pen over it and found I couldn't write my name on it, or a number, or anything else anyone could follow. But a blank card still tells someone that a person is on the other side of the door and is not going to say who.

I wanted Hannah to know that much. I went out the back and around the building to the front step. Ashby, in the car at the curb with the lobby on a handheld screen, said in my ear, "They're at the door." I slid the card under it and ran for the car.

**Ten days later.** They came to my office together, Tom in a clean shirt and steel-toed boots with his cap in his hands, and Ruth in a cardigan with a ring of keys at her belt. Tom did not sit. He picked up my stapler, turned it over twice, and put it back exactly where it had been.

"I took the training session," he said. "Not the other part. Your people said I could say no to it, and I did. It showed me how that machine works, enough to know I couldn't build one in a hundred years." He looked straight at me. "I've got a boy named Danny I haven't seen in five years. He sends money through Ruth and never says where from. Somebody moved my name up before I went on your table. Did he have something to do with that?"

I had decided on the stairs that I would not lie. "Danny works for a company I run. He saw your name on a list and asked me to look at your file. He asked me not to say more than that, so I'd like to leave it there."

"Is he all right?"

"As far as I know. I'm doing what I can about the rest."

Tom nodded slowly. Ruth had not moved. "Mr. Himura," she said, "who are you?"

"Kenji Himura. I build things, mostly. I run this clinic because there wasn't anyone else I'd trust with it."

She looked at me for a long moment. "I believe that," she said. "It doesn't tell me anything."

"No, ma'am."

"No." She folded her hands. "Thank you. For my husband."

"I have one request. Please don't tell Hannah about Danny and me. She'd ask questions I can't answer yet."

Tom agreed at once. Ruth waited a second longer, then said, "Not yet, then." I understood that she was giving me time, not a promise.

"Son," Tom said, "I ran a shipyard crew for thirty years, and this building's pumps are two years behind on their service. You don't have anyone running the place. I'd like the job."

I flinched at the word, and I hoped he hadn't seen. "I'd be glad if you took it."

Ruth reached up and straightened my collar, which did not need it. "And who looks after you?" she said. When I did not answer, she nodded, as though she had expected nothing else.

I walked them to the stairs. As the door closed I understood that I had two more people to worry about, and that I did not want to stop.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Gunnery Sergeant Dominic Vance*

### *The green book, 2051 to 2059*

**Summer 2051.** The hospice room had one window and one door, and Vance had lain in it for three weeks learning every sound the corridor made. A young man opened the door on a Monday and asked what he could teach. Vance said he had nothing to pay with but what was in his head, and the young man said that was what he wanted.

When Vance woke from the pod he could breathe to the bottom of his chest, and his first order was to send for Ashby, a radio technician from his old squad in Duluth, because someone had to hold the radio. He bought a green notebook that afternoon and began to write down every site and every threat to it.

**Winter 2051 to spring 2052.** He brought in the first squad of fighters that winter. In the spring, a few months before Byron Pyrrus signed, he added a second squad and three people from the intelligence services, who started reading court records to find out who might buy.

**November 2052.** His rule was that someone must always watch the road beyond the fence, since a wall only helps if you see what is coming up to it. A recruit named Danny Shepard, who read a building by where it carried its weight, was posted to Galveston as site engineer and guard lead for the hidden wing. Vance liked him at once. He never had to be told twice to walk a floor before standing on it.

**Summer 2053.** Three clinics opened: Galveston first, then Marseille, then Singapore. The platoon was full by autumn. In 2054 Vance moved Danny to Houston, because Tom Shepard was running the Galveston building now, and Danny could not be seen by him.

**April 2055.** The first rich buyers came through the senator's friends, and they came in quietly by the back door. Nobody had to be shown a threat. The guards stood in the room, and the buyers understood what that meant.

That same spring the two machines Kenji had built himself were finished. They looked and moved like men, and they would not tire, and Vance stopped walking behind Kenji. He had walked behind him for years, and for a month he felt the empty space behind him.

**November 2055.** A client's doctor asked a network nurse how the treatment worked, and offered her money to tell him. She reported it that day. The doctor left the country within the week, and no one learned who his buyer was. Vance wrote it down and marked it the first time anyone outside had asked.

**June 2057.** Five Level 2 pods vanished from a truck on the road between two clinics. The transfer order had been forged. Droids do not ride on public roads, so only human guards were with the load.

Ashby told him afterward that the alarm should have sounded and had not, and he said it flatly, writing it in the log, and Vance saw that he was blaming himself. Kenji upgraded every security droid that autumn.

**January 2058.** Oyama's pods went on sale to the public under Oyama's own name. A law firm acting for a buyer no one could name offered to buy Oyama's patents, all of them. Byron Pyrrus said no and telephoned Kenji the same hour.

Vance read the offer twice. It came from a law office he could not search.

**April 2058.** A second offer arrived, with a forged safety audit behind it that claimed Oyama's plants were unsafe. Byron refused again. Vance had the plants walked, one by one, and found nothing wrong, and that was the worst thing about it. A fence could not stop a forged audit.

**October 2058.** A third offer. Marchetti sat across a conference table from the buyer's lawyer, who told her that people who refuse for a third time meet "Cerberus." She said nothing at the table.

That night she drove to the warehouse and told Vance the word, and told him it had been said as a threat. He wrote it in the green book, and he did not know yet whether it was the name of a man, a company, or a gang.

**November 2058.** Three men came over the Galveston wall at night. The droids stopped them in the corridor before they reached the hidden wing. Vance sat with them in the cellar until dawn, and none of them said a word to him, not even their names. He did not expect them to.

**Spring 2059.** Vance was in Duluth that night, at Ashby's mother's table, when the call came. A delivery van that should have come to Galveston never arrived, and a different van took its place. The gate guard had been bought. Danny Shepard had been on overwatch in the car park, outside Hannah's roster, and the camera showed him seeing the van riding low and running at it.

He got the driver's door open before it went up, short of the east wing. If it had gone off at the building, no one on the lawn would have walked out. Danny's term would have ended that autumn, and he had meant to stay.

Two weeks after the funeral Kenji had a layer put into every member of the cadre that made selling him out something they could not do. Vance asked to be left out of it, and Kenji agreed.

He opened a clean page in the green book. He wrote at the top, *Who leads Cerberus.* Under it he drew a line and left the rest of the page empty, because he had a name and no face and no idea yet where to look.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (23 and 25)*

### *Galveston, 2054 and 2056*

**November 2054.** The man across the table finished everything on his plate, down to the parsley. Hannah watched him do it, because nobody ate like that unless they had once gone without. Her mother had set his place beside her father's, and he sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the kitchen door, in a house where nobody was going to attack him over pot roast.

A fifth place was laid at the end of the table, with the plate turned upside down and a napkin folded beside it. Her brother Danny worked in Houston for a security contractor, gates and fences, and he had been home twice in five years. Her mother set his place every Sunday anyway. The new man looked at the empty chair for half a second longer than a stranger would, and he did not ask whose it was.

Through the front window, Hannah could see a gray sedan at the curb with two men in it. They had been there since five, and neither had come in, though her mother had gone out twice to ask them. It was the first time she had met the man her father worked for. She had refused the clinic's offer of training the year before and had been polite to it, and cold, ever since.

"What do you do when you're not at the clinic?" she asked.

"I build things." He put his fork down and squared it against the edge of his plate. "Mostly at night, in a rented bay with one good lathe."

"What sort of things?"

"Tools. Gauges, jigs, small parts that other things get made with. It's a lot less interesting than it sounds."

"You don't mention anybody," she said. "No wife, no brother, no town you miss. Most people who've been somewhere say so."

"Hannah," her mother said.

"It's all right, Ma'am," he said. "I just don't talk much about where I'm from."

Her father picked up the gravy boat and put it down again. "He's a good boss," he said, to no one in particular. "He pays on time. He lets me fix the pumps."

When the plates were empty the man stacked them and took them to the counter without being asked, and squared the pile before he sat down. Hannah went up to bed not liking him. She had not caught him in a lie, and that bothered her more than a lie would have.

**March 2056.** She had left the Navy in February, and her father had talked her into the security post. Three weeks in, she walked the building with Kenji on a Thursday with her father's tape measure in her jacket pocket. She had measured every door herself, because she trusted her own numbers more than anyone's floor plans.

"There's no license on the front wall," she said. "Nobody wears a name tag. The east wing has a door I'm not allowed past, and there are two gray frames in the back corridor that my father calls equipment, and the door crew is better trained than any clinic would pay for. Walk me through the license first."

"It's in a drawer," Kenji said. "It's real. I took it off the wall on purpose."

"Why?"

"Because it names an owner, and I'd rather not be found."

"By who?"

"I can't tell you that yet." He said it without hurry. "The door crew are good because I pay for good ones. The name tags are my doing, too. People who work here have been asked not to give their names to patients, and I'd rather you heard that from me."

She wrote it down. "And the gray frames?"

"I can't answer that."

"The two men who follow you from room to room and never sit down?"

"They're my guards." He glanced toward the door of the corridor, where they stood waiting a pace apart, as they did every day. "That's all I can say."

She looked at him until she saw that it was true and that he would say nothing more. "Which of these can you explain?" she said. "Anything else on this list?"

"Not today. Maybe later. I know that's not much."

"Later." She nodded slowly. "I'll remember you said that."

Then she asked whether she could retrain the door crew her own way, and he said yes without a pause, and by Friday it was done. That evening she took a sheet of paper from the drawer, folded it in thirds, and wrote the first line. *Why does he stand at the edge of every room?* It was a question she had decided not to ask him.

**June 2056.** The hospital chain's men came on a Tuesday morning with a letter on good paper and a tool bag. The letter said inspection. The bag said something else. Her father was in the back corridor, under the pipe above the east wing door, working a valve that had dripped since April.

Two men she had not hired stood by the wing door and would not look at her. At the far end, Kenji had one hand on the door frame, doing nothing, and his two quiet guards stood beside him. She took it for fear.

Hannah stepped between the men and the wing door.

"You're leaving," she said. "Now."

The nearer man opened the bag.

A steel pipe, taped at one end.

Her fist closed on the tape measure in her pocket.

"Darling," her father said, very softly. His hand went to his back pocket.

At the far end, Kenji had not moved.

The pipe came up.

Behind her, servos shrieked.

A hand closed around the hand that held the pipe. Gray metal.

The pipe dropped. A second metal hand caught it before it hit the floor.

The other man lunged. Joints clanged. He hit the floor face first, and a knee settled between his shoulder blades.

Cable ties snicked. One. Two.

Two gray shapes stood over the men.

Robots.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (37)*

### *Galveston, April 2057*

**April 2057.** Hannah measured the lid of the cooler with her father's tape before she would sign for it. The cooler had no maker's plate, no serial number, and no stamp on the hinges, so she wrote its length on the custody sheet in pencil. I watched from the monitor-room door with the wall behind me and the clock in plain view. It was twenty past nine, and the surgeon on the other side of the front desk had not taken her eyes off that clock since she walked in.

Dr. Nadia Okonkwo had driven forty minutes from the county hospital with one patient on her mind and one rule in her pocket. Her patient was the last name on the county kidney list, and at midnight the registry would move him off it unless a surgeon logged an operation that day.

His new kidney had been growing in our east wing from his own cells and would be ready at nine. Two years ago her list had run to two pages. The clinics had emptied it, one patient at a time.

My two guards stood by the door behind me, where they always stood. I did not look at the cooler. I looked at what it was for.

Tonight it was for one man, but by next month every hospital between here and Houston would want one, and we could grow more than the state's lists could hold. I had promised myself in a tire shop that nothing would spread until the people around me were ready. Last June's men with the pipe had shown me what that waiting was for.

"Walk me through where it's been," Hannah said. "There's a donor line here, and I'd like to know what to write on it. I don't mind a strange answer. I mind a blank."

"There's no donor," I said. "It was grown from his own cells, in this building, and it hasn't left that cooler since nine o'clock. You can write that."

She held the pencil still, and then she wrote *none*. She did not ask anything else, though the next question was plain on her face. I felt relieved, and I disliked that I did.

Dr. Okonkwo lifted the tag off the handle and read it. It carried the clinic's licensed name, a batch number, and a state approval stamp, and the line for the maker was empty. The stamp was real.

Senator Whitcombe had spent four years making sure the clinics' organ license would survive any inspection, and I had never asked her how. Okonkwo slid the tag into her coat pocket and asked nothing. I had noticed that when a person needs something badly enough, and the paperwork is in order, the only question left is whether it works.

Hannah signed at nine forty-one, and the cooler was out the door with hours to spare.

**Twenty to one.** The call had come at midnight. The patient was alive, with a new kidney. I sat at the steel table in the staff kitchen with my back to the wall, and Hannah put a plate of cold eggs in front of me without a word.

I ate all of it, down to the last corner of toast. When the plate was clean she was watching me with her chin on her fist.

"You finish everything," she said. "I saw it at my mother's table the first night. Nobody does that unless they've gone without."

"I didn't always have enough."

She nodded once and let it be. Then she said, "Last June, when the men came with the pipe, you stood at the far end of the corridor with your hand on the door frame. I took it for fear. Walk me through it."

"The two gray frames you've seen in the back corridor are droids," I said. "They're the clinic's guards. They moved the moment the pipe came up, because that's what they're for, and there was nothing left for me to do. I stood still so I wouldn't be in their way." I took a breath. "I should have told you a year ago, when you asked me to my face. You run this building's security, and I let your father call them equipment while you planned doors around two things you didn't know could move."

"Where did they come from?"

"I can't answer that. I'm sorry."

"I thought you were afraid."

"I wasn't afraid of the men. I was watching to see if you were clear of them, and you weren't, because you'd walked toward them." I squared my fork against the edge of the table. "If I'd told you the rest, you'd have known you didn't need to."

She was quiet for a while. Then she turned her empty plate a quarter turn on the steel. "I'll tell you something back, so it's even. I took this job because my father asked.

That's what I told everyone. The real reason was you. I wanted to know what you were hiding, and tonight you told me something true about this building without my asking twice. So I'm staying."

I had thought she took the job for her father's sake. She had taken it to look at me, and she had been looking ever since. Everyone else who came through the clinic looked at what it did. I found that I would least want to lose the one person who looked at me.

"Is there any more coffee?" she said.

I got up and found the pot.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (26)*

### *Galveston, autumn 2057*

The weight line on the manifest was blank, and the rest of the page was in Kenji's square capitals. Hannah had been reading blank lines on his paper for two years. This one had stopped being strange in June, when five Level 2 pods left a loading dock on a transfer order with every box filled in but the weight. Kenji's own orders had left that line empty ever since, so that whoever signed would have to weigh the crate and write the number in.

The crate on the quay was the first load of Oyama's Primitive pods for the distributor, with sale to the public set for January. Rafa Ibarra, the rigger, stood beside it in his company's exoskeleton, a steel frame that took the weight of a man and whatever he carried. Beyond him an Oyama robot arm, run from a panel by a man in a booth, tied down the next load. "Look at that," Rafa said in Spanish, tipping his head at it.

"It does in forty minutes what my crew of eight did in a day. The crew's down to three. I'm one of them, so I'm not complaining."

"How long can the sled lift that much?" she asked.

"Two tons, for twenty minutes. Then the battery's flat, and it falls on somebody." He grinned. "I need your name on my tally before the shift whistle, jefa. If you don't sign, my crew doesn't go home."

She weighed the crate on the dock scale and wrote the number in the box. She did not have the other number, which was the weight it was supposed to be. She called Kenji, who picked up on the second ring and asked what she needed.

"A weight," she said.

"I don't say weights on a phone. Ask me tonight."

"Then I'm going to have to hold this crate until tonight. The shift whistle blows in an hour."

"Sign for it as it is, and mark it held. I'll tell you the number tonight."

**That evening.** He was on the clinic roof, where he went when he wanted to be out of sight and in the open at once. He had the number written on a card and handed it to her without being asked. She compared it with her own and found they matched, and he gave her a pen and let her write the figure into the box herself. It was the first time he had let her correct his paper.

"Whoever took the pods in June filled in everything but the weight," he said. "They didn't know it. Nobody outside this building does. Check it every time."

"I will." She capped the pen. "I want to ask you something. Do you want me to stop asking you things? I will, if you say so."

"No." He said it before she finished. "When you stop asking, I won't know what you think."

"I think you're hiding something big," Hannah said, "and not for money."

"No. Not for money."

"Then I can work with that." She looked out at the dark water for a moment. "Have dinner with me. Somewhere that isn't my mother's table."

**Thursday.** The diner on Seawall Boulevard had a counter, four booths, and a window onto the Gulf. He sat across from her with his hands around a mug and asked her something, the first question about herself he had ever asked. "Was it hard to leave the Navy?"

She talked for forty minutes. She told him about the ship's cooks and the captain's dog, and the day she had understood she would not be going back, and he did not look at the clock once. At a corner table, two men in plain jackets had a pot of coffee in front of them and had not touched it. She took them for his security and did not mind.

At her car he said, "Your first ship was the *Kearsarge*. Your commanding officer was Lieutenant Commander Reyes, and he wouldn't let anyone sit down on watch." She had mentioned both once, in passing, an hour before. Nobody ever remembered those.

She kissed him once, briefly. He stood there with his hands at his sides.

"What do you need?" he said.

Hannah laughed so hard she had to lean on the car. "That," she said, when she could. "Again. Thursday."
