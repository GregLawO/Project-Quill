# Cheap Hands and Angry Ones

*“Within the decade came power that did not depend on fuel markets, machines that could raise a district from raw material in a season, and robots able to do almost any manual task. Each of these threw someone out of work or into debt. The builders met the new machines first with disbelief and then with orders. The labor disputes of the late 2060s are among the best documented events of the age. The strikers were furious about losing their jobs, exactly as people always have been, and the fact that the thing taking their jobs was a robot from nowhere hardly seemed to trouble them.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A3 · Gunnery Sergeant Dominic Vance (60)*

### *Marseille, 2062*

Vance wore the gray coat into the ward because it hid the pistol, and July in Marseille did not care what a man wore. The hospital was old stone under new paint, and the fourth-floor corridor smelled of bleach laid over boiled vegetables. Her room had a door to the corridor and a door to the utility stair, and a window painted shut along its bead. He read it the way he read every room, by where he would stand to hold it, and he did not like the stair door.

The visitor's chair stood square to the bed with its back to the corridor. He turned it a quarter turn, so that he could watch the door without turning his back on her. The woman in the bed had a plastic bracelet with a number and no name, a face worn down to the bone, and eyes that went from the corridor door to the stair door to the window and back in the same order each time. He knew the look from good sergeants. She was reading the room for a way out, and she saw him see it. He let her see that he approved.

It had taken a year to reach this chair. His people could read ground, but none of them could read paper, and the Tulsa theft had shown him what that cost. So he had gone through the staff files of every ministry that licensed a clinic, and one analysis had stood above the rest because it said what the tariff schedules left out. By the time he found the author she had stopped coming to work. The transfer form in his inside pocket needed her name on it before the day matron made her rounds at one, and after that the ward would close around her for another week. He was not sure she had a week.

“Mademoiselle Brooks. Your paper on the Ankara tariff schedules was the best thing your directorate produced last year. It was also the last, I understand, because you stopped coming in.”

“I am not receiving visitors.”

“You are receiving one.” He said it gently, with no apology in it. “There is a machine on the second floor. People go in as you are, and they come out as they were. I will not ask you to believe me. I will ask you to let it be tried.”

“What does it cost?” she said. She had plainly heard this speech before, from other men, and each of them had wanted her savings.

“Nothing you are carrying.” Then he let the silence sit for exactly as long as he had practiced it in the car. A clever person would take a pause as proof that he was holding something back, and she would be right, but not about the machine. “Afterward, a conversation. There is work, and it is the sort you are good at.”

She looked at the painted window for a long moment.

“Yes,” she said.

The form came out of his coat and she signed it with a hand that was steady, the way a hand is steady once the mind behind it has decided. He heard the matron's shoes in the corridor a minute later, and they went past the door.

He spent the next three days in a chair at the end of the second-floor hall, and on the third day the frosted doors opened. She walked out on her own legs and did not touch the wall. He had gone ahead to the glass door at the far end of the long corridor and stood with his back to it, hands in his pockets, his left hand closed around the tags of men who were no longer walking. From there he could see her come. She stopped short of him, and her pulse showed in her throat, strong and slow.

“Sixty more years, for a start,” he said. “Mademoiselle Brooks, what would you do with them?”

She did not answer it. “For the record, three men have made me an offer since I fell ill, and each of them wanted my savings,” she said. “You want nothing I am carrying, so someone else is paying. Who is giving this away, and what does it cost the one who gives?”

He had never once heard his own people ask it. He had chosen well, and he stood a little straighter in the doorway for knowing it.

“I can't answer that,” he said. “The man who can is in this city, and I'll take you to him once you can manage the stairs. You can put it to him yourself.”

A week later she climbed the stairs above a ship chandler in the old port, where the whole building smelled of tar and hemp. Kenji sat with his back to the wall, in the seat Vance had taught him, and he squared the folder on the desk before she was through the door. She noticed. Then she counted the exits, and he waited until she had finished.

“Mr. Himura,” she said.

“What do you need?” Kenji said. “Tell me what's missing from this room, and I'll see to it before we start.”

“A window that opens,” she said. “For fourteen months I was in a room where it was painted shut. I would like to know that I can leave a room without asking anyone.”

“There is one, behind you. The latch is oiled, and it opens onto the chandler's yard.” He waited while she looked. “If you want a second one, tell me which wall.”

She did not ask for a second. He squared the folder once more and spoke plainly.

“I have one job for you, and I'm not going to explain the reason yet. I want you to count what I own: every company, clinic, and account you can find. Then I want you to tell me what is missing from the count. Tell me what you need to start, and when you need it by.”

“For the record, a count with no stated purpose cannot be checked,” she said. “I will need every ledger, in the order it was kept. If the order is wrong, I will say so.”

“You'll have them. If one is missing, tell me which, and I'll find it.”

A robot stood dark in the stairwell as she left, and she passed it without a word, which told Vance she had seen it and would ask later.

When her steps had gone down to the street, Kenji looked at the closed door.

“She will run this one day,” he said.

Vance stood by the window. He had just helped hire the person who would take his seat, and his work would narrow to guarding the man she served. He waited to feel something about that.

His hand was still in his pocket.

The tags lay loose in his palm.

He did not mind.
