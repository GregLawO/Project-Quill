# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (26 and 27)*

### *Galveston, 2057 and 2058*

**December 2057.** The ring had no stone. Hannah turned it under the workroom lamp and could not read the inside of the band until she fetched the magnifier from the clinic's first-aid cabinet. Her name was cut there in square capitals, so small that each letter was as thin as a hair. The shelf behind the bench held tools and no photographs.

He had made it on his own lathe. He stood across the bench from her with his hands flat on it and said everything at once, like a man who had practiced.

"I want to marry you. Before you answer, I need to tell you what I can't give you. I have no family. I can't tell you where I'm from. There are rooms in my life you won't be shown for a long time, and some of them perhaps never." He moved the ring box until it sat square to the edge of the bench. "I will never say a false thing to you. That I can promise."

"Is that the best you have?"

"It's the part I'm sure of."

She looked at the ring. "One question. Have you loved anyone before?"

"No. I don't think anyone got close enough."

"I need a night," she said.

She did not sleep. At dawn she walked back across the car park and found him at the workshop door with the light still on behind him. He had not slept either.

"Yes," she said. "And I'm going to keep asking."

"I'd be sorry if you stopped."

**September 2058.** The hall on the seawall had two blocks of chairs with an aisle between them. On her side every chair was taken. On his side every chair was empty. Hannah stood in the side room with her father and looked through the door at the empty block.

"Is there anyone you wish were here?" she asked, when he came to find her in the last minute before the music.

"Your parents," he said. "They are."

Her brother Danny had come in on leave for the day. He had sat on her side, in the second row, in a suit that did not quite fit his shoulders. When the music started, he got up, crossed the aisle, and sat alone on the groom's side. Nobody had asked him to.

Two men in dark suits stood against the wall on that side. They did not sit, they did not eat, and they did not speak. She took them for his security. At the back door a man in a gray coat stood like a Marine and watched the room instead of the bride.

Her father walked her in with his wrench in his back pocket. At the altar Kenji looked at him and said, "Dad." Tom's hand stopped on the cap he was holding.

At the reception Ruth crossed the room to the groom and straightened his collar, which did not need it. "And who looks after you?" she asked.

"Can I find you a waiter, Mom? You haven't eaten."

Ruth looked at him for a moment. She saw that he had stepped around the question, and she let him. She did not ask again.

When the toasts began, Ruth stood and raised her glass toward the groom's side, where only Danny sat. Hannah had a question ready, one from the list in her drawer. She did not ask it. She raised her glass.

At the back door, the man in the gray coat raised his.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (40)*

### *A Gulf coast plant, spring 2060*

A thermos stood on the gatehouse ledge, and the guard had not touched it. The lid said EAT in block capitals. It was six in the morning, and Hannah was standing on the far side of the gate with her hands in her jacket pockets.

I had been sleeping at the plant more nights than not. It was a year since the bomb. Danny had died that night outside the building, and neither of us had said his name to the other since the funeral. We talked about errands. We did not talk about the child.

"I'm leaving the clinic job in June," she said. "I'm starting my own security firm. I wanted you to hear it from me and not from the roster."

"What do you need?"

"You to stop deciding what I can bear."

I did not answer at once. "I have been deciding it since the bomb."

"I know. Was it someone who wanted what you make?"

"Yes, I think so." I had practiced this for a year, but it still came out in the wrong order. "A group that calls itself Cerberus. They stole five of our machines in 2057. They tried three times to buy Oyama's patents in 2058, and the last time their lawyer said their name. Three men came over the Galveston wall that November, and they were theirs. I can't prove they did the bomb. I don't know who runs them."

She waited. She had always waited for the rest.

"There's more," I said. "The two men who walk behind me are machines. I built them. They look and move like men."

"I have nodded to one of them in the corridor for three years."

"I know. I should have told you sooner."

"What else?"

"Danny worked for me from 2052. Since 2056, he watched your back from the car park, because I asked him to. The night of the bomb he saw the van riding low and went after it."

She stood very still. Her thumb moved along the scar on her forearm. "That is the most you have ever told me."

"I know."

"I'm angry about Danny." She said it flatly. "I'm angry that he watched me for three years and I didn't know. I'm angry that you let me say his name at the funeral and said nothing."

"You should be."

She did not leave. The name Cerberus had sounded familiar to me since the day Marchetti first said it, and I still could not say where I had heard it. I did not tell her that.

"Someday I want to talk about the child," she said.

"I can't yet."

"Not yet is fine. Not never."

"Have you eaten?" I asked.

She laughed, once, and it surprised her. She reached over the gate, took the thermos from the ledge, and put it in my hands. I finished it standing there.

**An hour later.** The hall had been finished the week before. Oyama's Level 3 security droids stood along its walls. Inside, four things were being built to my drawings: a fusion reactor core, a data-crystal bank, a powered exoskeleton frame, and a Gundarium plate. My two Level 5 guards stood at the door, as they had for five years.

Hannah had taken them for men until an hour ago.

I walked the floor and looked at each machine. I asked what it was for, and then what it would be used for next.

The two-column sheet from the tire shop hung on the wall, with nine rows under PRIMITIVE and LEVEL 2. The yard lease lay open on the desk beside it. One line was blank: the name of the security contractor.

I had one name I wanted there, and it was hers. Writing it meant showing her the hall, and she had just asked me to stop deciding what she could bear.

I took the pen and held it over the line.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (42)*

### *A Gulf coast plant, 2062*

Vance's note on Brooks was on my desk, and I had read it four times. It was one page in his square hand. It said she had read ministry paper for six years, and that her paper on the Ankara tariff schedules had found what the schedules left out. It said she was twenty-four and dying in Marseille.

The second page was his list. It had four lines.

The forged transfer order of 2057, which took five pods. The forged safety audit of 2058. The van that replaced the Thursday van in 2059. The forged state letter of 2061, which closed the Galveston clinic.

I read the last line again. The letter had closed Galveston and nothing else. Senator Whitcombe's faction had stopped the other closures, and within weeks it had forced Galveston to reopen. The faction could undo paper after it landed. But nobody had seen the letter coming, and nobody had seen any of the other three.

Vance guarded doors. I had built a network around bodies and walls, and each time the enemy had walked in through paper. I needed someone who read paper the way Vance read ground.

I opened the drawer where I kept the other file.

I had opened it on the night of the bomb. It was meant for the people responsible. It had one line in it, which was Danny's name. Nothing else had gone into it in three years. I looked at the name and shut the drawer. It was a habit by now.

Vance came in at nine and stood by the door, because he never sat in my office. "You read it?" he said.

"Four times." I squared the two pages against the edge of the desk. "Why her?"

"Because she counts. She saw what was missing from a page, and nobody else in the ministry did. I can guard a room. I can't read one."

"She's dying."

"Yes."

"Then she will say yes."

I took a pen from the tray and wrote one word across the top of his note, in the plain hand I used for orders.

*Approved.*

"What do you need?" I asked.

"Nothing. A car, and three days." He picked up the note. "She'll want to know who you are."

"I'll tell her when she's well."

He folded the note once and put it inside his coat. He left. I sat with my hands flat on the desk and looked at the closed drawer.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Teodor Ashby (33)*

### *The test range, 2063*

The range log had a line at the bottom for the signature. Ashby had not signed it. The crews rotated at noon tomorrow. It was a quarter to eleven, and he was still walking the line of machines with the stethoscope in his hand.

"Listen to that," he said.

The droid beside him stood with its arm half raised. Ashby pressed the stethoscope against its elbow and held still. Inside the joint a bearing was ticking every fourth beat, under the main hum. It was too quiet to matter today. It would matter in a week.

"Pull it," he said. "I'll write it up."

"It's within tolerance," said the crew chief.

"It is. Pull it." He wrote the unit number in the log and the sound beside it, in words. *Ticking, every fourth beat. Within tolerance. Replace before next rotation.* Then he moved on to the next machine.

He had written every fault he heard in writing for fourteen years. Once, in a trench, a relay radio had drifted half a beat off its timing. He had heard it and decided it was nothing. He had told no one. A few hours later the squad was gone. Since then he reported everything, whether anyone wanted it or not.

At eleven twenty the hull went up.

It was a plain gray cylinder, unmanned, with the drive in the stern. The crews had loaded it that morning with instruments only. Ashby stood in the control shed with the headset on and the stethoscope around his neck, and he did what he did with every machine: he listened.

The engines on the old test rigs had always made noise. They whined, they ticked, they shook the floor. The faults were in the noise. He had spent his life learning to hear what was wrong inside it.

The hull did not make any.

He heard the crews breathe. He heard the fan in the shed. On the headset, from the instruments aboard the hull, he heard nothing at all. There was no hum, no drift, and no click. The channel was open and clean, and it was empty.

He took off the headset and put it back on. The channel was still empty.

"Telemetry's perfect," said the technician at the next desk. "Not one dropout."

"I know," said Ashby.

"That's good, isn't it?"

Ashby did not answer. He kept his eyes on the instruments. A machine that never complained was hiding something. He had learned that in the trench, and he had never stopped believing it. He watched the readout go flat and steady, hour after hour. Nothing on it moved.

The hull came back the next morning. Every system had run clean. The drive had run clean. The log was due.

He sat down with the pen and wrote the entry plainly, because that was what he did.

*The instruments recorded no sound from the drive at any point in the flight. Every machine I have tested makes some. I do not know why this one does not.*

The crew chief read it over his shoulder. "You can't sign off on that."

"I'm not signing off that it works," Ashby said. "I'm signing off that I wrote it down."

He signed. The crews rotated at noon.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (43)*

### *A Gulf coast plant, 2063*

The flight log was on my desk in my own hand, and I had squared the pages twice. The first unmanned hull had flown a measured light year in a day. It had gone from the yard to a point beyond Neptune's orbit and come back. I had kept the test as small and as quiet as I could. There had been a handful of staff, Ashby's crews, and no one else.

I had known it would work. I had known every step of it before the hull left the ground. What I had not known was how it would feel to watch a machine do a thing nobody had done.

Ashby's range log was clipped to the back. His last entry was in plain words, and I read it again. *No sound from the drive at any point.* He had signed it anyway, with the line written out. I had never known him to trust a quiet machine, and I understood that he did not trust this one. I did not argue with him. I wrote nothing in reply.

Under the log was Brooks's first report.

She had been with us for a year. It was twelve pages, with a map of cutouts, and it listed the customers Cerberus was known to supply. She had left two lines blank. She had written beside each one: *Cause unknown.* I liked that she had not guessed.

Three names on the customer list stopped me. Hahne-Kedar. Ariake. Sirta.

I knew them. I had read them somewhere, or heard them said, and I could not say where. They were company names, and they meant nothing I could point to. I took a pencil and made a mark beside each. Then I turned to a clean page and wrote the three names at the top.

It was the start of a list. I did not know yet what the list was for.

I washed my hands in the sink at the back of the room and dried them. Then I sat down again with the sales release in front of me.

The release was one page. It said that the drive could be sold, and it had one line left empty for the first customer's name. I had written the drive's specifications on the sheet myself. It was what I had built it for.

I asked what it was for. It was for reaching another star.

I asked what it would be used for next. The list was longer, and it was the same list as every machine I had built. Someone would buy it to carry cargo. Someone would buy it to carry weapons. Someone would try to take it from me.

I picked up the pen. The line on the release stayed blank.

I held the pen over it.
