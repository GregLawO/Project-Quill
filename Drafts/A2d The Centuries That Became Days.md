# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Maya Brooks (25)*

### *Marseille, 2063*

Three forms lay side by side on her desk, and each had one blank line. Maya Brooks had read them four times. The forms came from three ministries in three countries, and each cleared the sale of one hull. The line that was empty on all three read *Owner of record.*

She counted what she could. Three stamps, three signatures, three dates. Each form had been filed on a Monday and stamped nine days later. The stamps were real. She had checked each one against the ministry's own seal.

Her office was a rented room above a ship chandler, with one window and a door that locked from the inside. She had asked for the room because it had two exits. It smelled of tar and rope. Below her a man was arguing the price of a coil of cable, and she could hear every word.

She had one small want that morning. She wanted the three forms matched to the three hulls before Kenji's call at noon. She had matched them. What she had not done was stop looking at the blank lines.

A forged audit had nearly closed Oyama's plants. Cerberus had beaten the network with paper three times, and each time the weakness was something no one had thought to check. She did not like a blank that nobody had explained.

The phone rang at noon.

"What do you need?" Kenji said. He always said it first.

"Why is the owner line empty on all three?"

"Because I told Marchetti to leave it empty." He spoke slowly. "If a ministry stamps a form with a name on it, someone can follow that name. If the line is empty, there is nothing to follow. The designs belong to the foundation, but I don't want its name on a form a ministry will file."

"The ministries didn't ask."

"No."

"I counted the days. Each took nine. They are three different countries, with three different clerks, and each one took nine days to the day." She turned the second form over, as if the back might say something else. "They didn't ask because someone asked them not to. The senator's faction did that for us. I can see the pattern, and I only had three forms. If I can count it, so can anyone else who is reading our paper."

He did not say anything for a while. She waited. She was good at waiting for the second half of an answer.

"Then they will see it," he said.

"Yes."

"What do you need?"

"Three forms that don't match," she said. "Different days, different clerks. I'll have a list by tonight."

When the call ended she took her notebook from the drawer and wrote one line. *Three forms. Nine days each. We left a blank, and we left a pattern.* She looked at the second part for a long time. She had not intended to write it.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (33)*

### *A Gulf coast yard, 2064*

The checklist for the day's test ran to two pages in her own hand. Hannah went down it with a pencil, the way she had every morning for two years. Fences. Gates. The roof, where the Level 3 sentries stood. The line of human guards between the hull and the road. She marked each item as she touched it.

What was not on the page bothered her more. The hull on the gantry had no maker's plate. No technician stood beside it. The readout on the panel was a column of symbols she could not read, and the one man she had asked to explain it had said he was not the person to ask.

She had seen this before. Two years ago her firm had guarded its first job, a single unmanned frame bolted to a gantry. She had written down the same three gaps then. No plate, no technician, no readout. When the frame lifted off the gantry, the hum it made had dropped by half a tone. She had written that down too, and then looked for the technician who could tell her why. Nobody could find him.

She wrote it again now at the top of her pre-flight note. *Hum drops half a tone at lift. Nobody has been able to explain why.*

She also had a standing instruction. If any man at the fence said the name Cerberus, she was to hear it first. In four years nobody had.

The sun was up. The line of guards stood where she had put them, a pace apart. She walked it once more with her father's tape measure in her pocket. At the far end of the yard she stopped and looked at the hull.

"Clear," her deputy said on the radio.

The hull began to hum. It rose off the gantry with no sound but that, a steady note she could feel in her teeth. At midpoint the note fell half a tone and stuttered.

The gantry shrieked.

A clamp sheared. A bolt whipped across the yard.

Hannah ran.

A guard stood in its path, one hand still on his radio. She hit him at the knees. They went down together.

The bolt struck the wall behind them.

She did not move. Her hand was on the guard's collar, and the tape measure was digging into her hip.

Above them, the hum came back. It rose. The note steadied.

The hull went up through the light and was gone.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Emil Varga*

### *The Lisbon docks, 2065*

The price of a day's work had been chalked on the board outside the harbor office, wiped off, and chalked again twice that week. Emil Varga read it from the gate. It was lower than it had been in the spring. He had a crew of twelve who wanted paying, and the agent's office closed at five.

He needed the agent's signature on the crew's chits before that. It was his one want that day, and he was not going to get it. A crowd stood on the quay between the gate and the office, three deep, and the police had run a rope across the middle of it.

"What's going on?" he asked a man in a dock apron.

"A hull's going up from the east berth. They say it's going to the other side of the Solar System and back before dark."

"Nobody sails to the other side of the Solar System."

"This one does."

Emil was a foreman, and he had watched machines replace men on that quay for five years. First came the cranes, then the arms, then the robots that tied down loads without a crew. He knew what each one cost, and he knew how many men it had taken. He had never had a reason to look up at one.

His daughter was holding the end of his sleeve. She was six. She had asked at breakfast whether she could come, and he had said she could stay at the gate while he worked. She had not stayed at the gate.

"Papa, I can't see."

He looked at the crowd and then at the agent's window. The blind was down. The agent would not sign anything until the hull had gone. Nothing would happen at the office until then, and everyone in the crowd knew it.

He picked her up and sat her on his shoulders. She weighed more than she had at Easter. He felt it in his back, and he had stopped being surprised by it.

A note began under the quay. It was a hum, low and steady, and it came up through his boots. The crowd went quiet. A gray hull lifted from the east berth with nothing under it and no flame, and it rose straight up.

His daughter's hands were in his hair. "Where is it going?"

"I don't know."

"Will it come back?"

"They say before dark."

The hull was a speck, and then it was gone. People did not cheer. They stood with their faces up and waited for the sky to say something. He noticed that his own throat had gone tight.

"Papa," his daughter said. "I want to go."

"Where?"

"Wherever that was."

He laughed, once, and it hurt. He had not wanted to go anywhere in years. He held her ankles and stayed where he was, with the crew's chits folded in his pocket and the sun in his eyes, until the rope came down.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Colm Farrady*

### *Houston, 2065*

The application on his desk was for a loan of eleven hulls, and the firm that wanted them had existed for eleven months. Colm Farrady read the date twice. He had been a lender for nineteen years, and no lender he knew would have touched a company that young.

The company was not the borrower. The borrower was a shipping line, and it was ordinary. The company was the seller. It had no history and no offices he could find, and the drive in the hulls it was selling had flown for two years.

His want was simple. The credit committee closed the quarter on Friday, and he needed the loan signed before it did. If he signed, the line got its hulls and the bank got its fee. If he did not, a competitor down the street would take it by lunch.

He did what he always did when something was too good. He read what each party owned. The borrower owned its docks, its lease, and its debt. The hulls would own the line's future. He turned to the seller's form.

*Owner of record.* The line was blank.

He had seen blanks before. A clerk forgot, or a lawyer wanted something hidden. This one was different. Every other line on the form had been filled in by the same careful hand, down to the postal code. This line alone had been left empty, and nobody had put a mark in it to say why.

"Get me the registry," he said to the girl at the next desk.

She got it on the second try. The registry was a public file, and he went through it line by line. The seller's incorporation was eleven months old. Its shares were held by a trust, and the trust was held by a foundation. The foundation's name appeared on the page at last, in plain type.

*Tallis Bay.*

He had never heard of it. He searched his own records and found nothing. He searched the registry for the foundation and found it on a second page. It had been filed in December of 2051.

He worked it out on the back of the form. That was fourteen years ago. It was two years before the first hull flew, and long before anyone had known there would be a drive to sell. Somebody had made a foundation to own it before the thing existed.

He looked for an address and found a filing agent's office. He looked for a trustee and found a name he had seen on legal letterhead in three cities, and it told him nothing. He looked for an owner, and the line stayed empty.

He wrote the foundation's name on the form, in the blank. Then he crossed it out. It was not his line to fill.

The loan was still on his desk, and the quarter closed on Friday. He had a name now. It led nowhere, and it was older than the machine.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Hannah Shepard (35)*

### *A Gulf coast yard, 2066*

The Halyard's registration card was a small plastic rectangle with a stamp in one corner. Hannah held it up to the light on the yard's gangway and looked at the empty line. *Owner of record.* There was nothing on it. There had been nothing on every card she had seen for four years.

Her firm had guarded eleven departures from this yard. Tonight's was the first with a paying cargo, and the first she would follow out. She had two cutters on the water, a crew of eight on each, and a clear sky. On the pad the hull stood on its gantry with the lamps burning white.

Her pre-flight note was on a clipboard. She had written it at four that morning, and she read it again. *Hum drops half a tone at lift. No technician. No plate. The drive never complains.* The last line was something a man named Ashby had said to her once, in a corridor, and she had put it on every note since. She did not know what it meant. She had stopped looking for someone to ask.

"Fences clear," her deputy said on the radio. "Roof clear."

"Walk me through the cargo."

"Sealed containers, and a manifest with every box filled in. The weight is on it, in the signer's hand." He paused. "The owner line is blank."

"It is on all of them."

She went down the gangway and stood at the foot of the gantry. The crew had cleared the pad. Beyond the fence a few dozen people had gathered with their faces turned up. Nobody had asked them to come. They were there because the ships had stopped being a rumor.

The hum started.

It rose off the gantry on the note she knew. At the half-tone drop it fell and recovered, and this time nothing sheared. The hull climbed. She watched it through the glass of the cutter's bridge, and then through the cutter's radar, and then through nothing.

"Hold the follow," she said.

"Holding. We have it at ten thousand meters."

"Twenty."

"Twenty. Thirty."

The radar screen showed a single bright dot, climbing steadily. Her hand was on the rail.

The dot disappeared.

It did not fade. It did not drift. It was on the screen, and then it was not.

The cutter lurched. The deck came up under her feet. A wall of air struck the bridge, and every window rang. She was thrown against the rail. Someone shouted.

She got up. The screen was blank. The sky was empty.

"Find it," she said.

"There's nothing to find."

"Find it."

Her deputy looked at her. He did not say what both of them knew. She held the registration card in her fist, and the empty line pressed into her palm.
