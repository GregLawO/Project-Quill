# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (30)*

### *Texas City, 2050*

The pencil finished the line before he knew he was holding it. It was a long line, ruled without a ruler, and it ended exactly where the one beside it began, in the corner of a sheet taped flat across a kitchen table he did not recognize. He set the pencil down slowly, the way a man sets down something that might go off, and looked at his right hand as if it belonged to someone else.

The flat was two rooms and a bath above a tire shop, close enough to the ship channel that the window glass hummed whenever a tanker sounded its horn. Gulf humidity had swollen the door frame, and a window unit rattled in the wall, losing its fight with the heat. Everything in the room had a use, and he read the uses in the order he met them: a kettle for heat, a chair for sitting or for wedging a door, a lamp with eleven feet of cord. He knew the cord was eleven feet without measuring it, which unsettled him, and what unsettled him more was that he could not tell which of them had known it first.

The body was the larger problem. It stood an inch taller than he expected, and the hands were a stranger's, thick through the palm, with a welder's burn shining pale across the left thumb. In the bathroom mirror a man of thirty looked back, Japanese, lean, unshaven, with eyes that held steady. He met the stranger's gaze until the stranger blinked first, which settled nothing but told him the face would do what it was told.

He came back to the table and made himself look at the page properly. The drawing was a cross-section in a draftsman's fine line, a long enclosed cradle with channels running down its length, fields marked in arrows, and a tank at its foot with a feed he could trace without trying. It was a thing built to hold a body still while something worked on it, and he understood that the way he had understood the cord, through the hands, with nothing in his head to explain it. A welder's thumb had not drawn these lines, and a welder's hands did not hold a pencil this lightly.

On the counter sat a bowl of cold rice with half an egg gone hard on top, left by a man who had meant to come back to it. He ate it standing, every grain, and scraped the bowl clean with the spoon. Leaving food had never been something he could afford, and he saw no reason the rule should lapse with the body.

There were old words for what might have happened to him, and he did not say any of them, not even in an empty room. He made a list instead, because lists were what he had. The rent was paid through the month, and the refrigerator held three days of food. A calendar on the wall was marked with yard shifts in a small, careful print that leaned backward.

No one had missed him yet. He weighed the people who might believe him and found the list empty, then weighed the people who might take an interest in a man with a head full of things he had never learned, and found that list worse.

Someone knocked at eleven, three raps, patient. The man outside was big, judging by the way the boards took his weight.

"Himura? You in there? We're a man short on Thursday, and the crew keeps asking for you. Say the word and the shift is yours."

He did not answer. His shoulders had drawn up toward his ears, and he made them come down again. In the building where he had lived before, footsteps had passed his door every evening and not one pair had ever stopped; this man had stopped, and had asked for him by name, and being asked for sat badly in his chest.

Himura. Kenji Himura. He said it once under his breath, to see whether it would fit. Boots went down the stairs, one flight, and the tire shop's side door banged.

He went back to the table and laid the calendar beside the sheet. The shifts were printed small and leaning backward, the hand of a careful man who trusted nobody to read his writing. The lettering on the diagram stood upright, in square, unhurried capitals, and nothing about it matched. The capitals were his own hand, the one he had carried in with him, and the body had reached past its own habits to use it.

At the head of the sheet, above the cradle and the tank, one word stood alone, underlined twice.

It was a name. He could tell from the way it was written, the way a man letters the title of something he means to build.

He said it aloud. The sounds came out whole and meant nothing.

He could say it. He could not read it.

His right hand had already found the pencil.
