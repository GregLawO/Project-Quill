# The Centuries That Became Days

*“Then, in the 2060s, ships appeared that could cross to another star in days. The papers of the time give their pace as about a light year a day. Alpha Centauri lies a little over four light years from Sol, so the trip took four days or so. Until that decade no human being had gone beyond the Solar System, and Alpha Centauri had been, for every earlier generation, a destination fit only for the dreams of grandchildren’s grandchildren. The chronicles show crowds at the first departures, and governments scrambling to understand what had been placed in their hands.”*

— *The Road to the Stars*

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (30)*

### *Texas City, 2050*

The lathe had stopped, and Kenji could not remember switching it off. A bar of tool steel sat between the centers, cut to a taper he had not planned, still warm under his palm and smelling of hot iron and cutting oil. He turned it over twice and found nothing wrong with it, which was the part that never stopped being strange.

The rear bay of the tire shop had been his since spring, rented for cash from an owner who asked nothing as long as the rent came on the first. The lathe was a scrapped yard unit with a cracked bed, hauled here in pieces on a borrowed trolley and scraped true by hand over six weekends. Beside it stood a surface plate he had lapped flat, a vise bolted to a beam he had checked with a level, and a pegboard of gauges hung in the order he had made them. Through the roller door came the smell of the ship channel, brine and diesel, laid over rubber and oil.

Seven months had passed since the pencil, and he still thought of that morning in pieces. The line that finished itself. The stranger's hands, the stranger's face in the mirror, the cold rice he had eaten to the last grain because that was how he ate. He had spent the week after testing the body the way a man tests ice, one careful step at a time, asking the hands for things he had never learned, and the hands had answered every time.

By the end of that week he had stopped testing, because the hands had never once been wrong. He wrote nothing down. A man who kept notes on a thing like this was a man who could be read, and there was no one to tell, since no one would believe him and anyone who took an interest would be worse.

He had taken the Thursday shift. The foreman's offer had come through his door that first morning, three patient raps, and being asked for by name had sat badly in his chest, but a man who stayed in his flat was a man who got noticed. The crew called him Himura and then quiet, and he let them. The yard paid for the bay one shift at a time.

His hands were already back at the bar, and he let them go. They squared it in the chuck, set the tool, and began the next pass while his mind wandered back over the months, which was how most of the bench had been built. He did not decide, as a rule. He sat down meaning to think, and an hour later there was a gauge where the hour had been.

He read the bench the way he read everything, by what each piece was for and what it would be used for next. The surface plate existed to check the lathe. The lathe existed to cut the gauges, and the gauges existed to cut finer tools, each a little truer than the last, each one step nearer to something none of them could make yet. The whole bench was a ladder, and he had been climbing it for seven months without once looking up.

He looked up. The sheet from that first morning hung over the vise, pinned at the corners and browned by the bay's damp, the cradle and the tank drawn in a draftsman's fine line. Tonight the work lamp had slipped on its arm and thrown its light across the paper at a low angle. Down the left edge, where he had always taken the ruling for a border, the ruling had a texture, small and regular, like writing.

He stepped closer.

The ruling was not ruling.

It was a column of small capitals, a hand's width from the edge, in the same square hand as the name.

Surface plate. Lathe, scraped true. Bench vise. Gauge set. Taper reamers.

He ran a finger down it.

The finger stopped at the line the bar in the chuck would finish tonight.

Every line above it was done.

Seven months he had called it improvising.

He squared the sheet on its pin. Squared it again.

Under tonight's line, there was one more.

It was the word at the head of the sheet.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (31)*

### *Texas City, 2051*

The dog stood up on a hip that should not have held it. Kenji kept one hand on the lid as it finished rising and watched the animal test the leg, weight forward and weight back, the way he had once tested the body. It had come off the channel road at four that morning with its pelvis in pieces, and he had carried it in across both arms because he did not know what else a person did. Now it sneezed at the smell of the coolant and walked out of the cradle on four sound legs.

The machine filled the back half of the bay, a cradle of ceramic and printed steel under a glass-clear lid, with the tank behind it breathing through its filters. He had not designed it so much as followed it. The sheet from the first morning had carried only the early lines, and the hands had gone on down the ladder for a year, past the lathe and the gauges and the tooling, until the ladder ended in this. He could read the word at the head of the sheet now, and it said what the thing was.

He read the machine the way he read everything, by what it was for and what it would be used for next. It was for rebuilding a body. Next it would be used by whoever held it, on whoever they chose, and every government, hospital chain, and criminal who heard of it would come to be that person. He looked at the roller door and the hardware-store padlock on its hasp, and the size of the problem arrived all at once.

The machine could not leave the room.

Something had to, though. A cure that no one saw cured no one, and a man who sat on a miracle in a tire shop was a man waiting to be found. He pulled a stool to the bench, and the hands began a second, cruder design before he had decided to draw it.

The new model mended and did not renew. Every part could be bought, ground, or wound by a good shop with a decade to spare. The question he put to each part was the only one that mattered: could the world have made this without him?

He ruled a clean sheet into two columns and pinned it above the vise, beside the old one. He headed the left column Primitive and the right Level Two, wrote the cruder machine under the first and the true one under the second, and drew a line beneath both. Under the line he wrote a single rule. What leaves the room must be something the world could have invented without him, or the question of who built it will have an answer.

By October a medical-equipment distributor in Houston had set up a demonstration in its own loading bay. The cruder unit sat on a pallet under the sodium lights with a deckhand’s broken forearm in its cradle, and the distributor’s man watched the clock for forty minutes. When the lid lifted, the deckhand flexed his fingers and swore softly in wonder.

“Could it take ten years off a man?” the distributor’s man asked. “I have clients who would pay more for that than for a new hip.”

“No. It mends what is broken, and it stops there,” Kenji said. “Sell it as exactly that, and it will be worth more to you for being honest.”

The man smiled the way a man smiles at a price, and wanted the rights. The distributor’s lawyer arrived a day early to settle terms. Kenji took him through to the front office, where they sat among the tire racks and the lawyer uncapped a pen.

“Before my client commits a dollar, I need the owner of the design,” he said. “Whoever it is signs the license, and whoever it is takes the liability if a patient sues. Give me the name and I’ll have it drafted by Friday.”

Kenji had prepared for questions about the machine, but the lawyer wanted only a name. “Tallis Bay Medical Systems,” he said. “It’s new, and I’m its only officer, so I’ll sign for it. It holds every drawing and every part of the design, and it will go on holding them whether I’m here or not.”

The lawyer wrote it down without looking up.

Kenji watched the pen finish the last word. He had made the name up that morning at the sink, and he had not checked a map or a register to see whether it was taken. The lawyer had not checked either. A name cost nothing, and it would be used next by anyone who came looking for him, to find whoever stood behind it, and so far that was one man in a tire shop.

When the lawyer had gone, he went back to the bay and stood facing the tarp. Under it the true machine sat quiet, worth more than the building and everything on the street, and it needed a guard he could not hire. A guard was a person, and a person could be asked questions. He cleared the corner bench and laid out an actuator set and a frame, and the hands began to build a sentry.

The agreement came on Friday by courier.

Eleven pages. A yellow tab marked the last.

He carried it to the bench. The tarp stood behind him. Under it the true machine ticked as its coolant settled, and in the corner the half-built sentry waited without a head.

The signature block read *Tallis Bay Medical Systems, by:*

The line under it was empty.

He washed his hands. Twice. Dried them.

He squared the page to the edge of the bench. Squared it again.

He uncapped the pen.

His hand came down and stopped a hair above the paper.

It did not go on.

It did not go back.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (32)*

### *Baytown, 2052*

The buzzer went off at twenty to three.

It was a washing-machine buzzer, bolted to a sheet of plywood with drywall screws, and in the closed mezzanine it was loud enough to rattle his teeth. Kenji came off the cot with the blanket still in one fist. Below him a chain hit the concrete and slithered.

He found the work lamp on the second try. The console was the thing he had rigged in one weekend: four salvaged monitors stacked two and two, a strip of indicator lamps soldered to perfboard, the buzzer, and a bundle of wire running down through the floor to the controllers. One lamp burned red, and it was the dock door. The three beside it were amber, which meant the sentries were awake and had not yet been asked to do anything.

On the monitors, four men in black rolled under the dock door, flashlights low, one with a pistol along his thigh. The warehouse was a sheet-metal shell on a gravel lot east of Houston, rented that spring, with pallets of tire stock along the walls and three long crates standing open by the east wall. In the middle of the floor, under a canvas tarp, sat the real machine, worth more than the building and everything on the road.

Each sentry was a grey frame with a task controller behind its chest plate. The controller had one job: if a man crossed the yellow line Kenji had painted ten feet inside the dock, take him down and bind him without killing him. Kenji had built all three over the winter, and none of them had ever met a living person.

The men did not search. They did not look at the pallets. They crossed the yellow line without looking down, heading for the tarp like men who had been shown a map.

“Worth a hospital,” one muttered. “That’s what he said.”

“You ever meet him?”

“Never. Get the tarp.”

The first sentry moved.

No sound. No warning. It stood in its corner, and then it stood between them and the tarp.

The pistol came up.

A grey hand closed on the wrist. The pistol left the man’s fingers and sat in the sentry’s other palm.

The second man charged. The second sentry turned a shoulder. He went down hard, face to the concrete, and a knee settled in the small of his back.

Kenji’s hand found the stack of monitors. Squared it. Squared it again.

The third sentry took the man at the tarp by both arms. The fourth man bolted for the dock. The first sentry crossed forty feet in four strides and took him by the collar.

Nobody fired. Nobody bled.

Four men lay face down in a row. Cable ties snicked, one, two, three, four.

None of them had reached the tarp.

Then he did the rest from a list. He called the county sheriff on a prepaid phone bought for cash, gave the address and the word *burglary* and nothing more, and put the phone in the drainage ditch behind the lot. The sentries carried the four men out to the loading dock and sat them against the wall, then walked back to their crates and stood in them. He closed the lids and sent the stand-down, and each frame went dark with a small click. Four men would tell a deputy about grey machines, and the deputy would write down *intoxicated*, and by the time the headlights turned onto the gravel the floor held only pallets and a tarp.

He washed his hands at the utility sink in the back, and when they were clean he washed them again.

He was quietly pleased with himself, which was not a feeling he trusted. But under the pleasure lay a debt to a man he had met twice. Last autumn, in a hospice room that smelled of bleach, a retired Marine named Dominic Vance had lain with failing lungs and a nurse’s card taped above the bed.

“I’ve got nothing to pay you with,” Vance had said, one breath at a time. “Whatever you’re selling, it would be what’s in my head.”

“That’s all I’m asking for,” Kenji had said. “I would copy it out and give you your breath back in the same hour. I can’t tell you what I would do with it.”

Vance had studied the ceiling a while. “Do it,” he said. “I’ve got nothing else anyone would take.”

All winter Kenji had run the sentries through the sergeant’s engagements until they moved as he had moved, and tonight the second sentry had put a man on the floor with the sergeant’s own knee and the sergeant’s own thumb on the pulse at the wrist. Kenji knew both. He had watched them from the inside.

The sentries had worked, but they had worked late. They had stopped four men only after the four men were inside, and no one had warned him the four were coming, and no one was watching the road they came by. A lock tells you about a man only once he is touching it.

He needed eyes beyond the fence. Eyes were people, and people could not be built in a shed over a winter. They had to be found, and he had one name.

He did not trust Vance from acquaintance, having met him only at the treatment and the upload. But the sergeant was the only living man who knew what the machine did, and he had told no one. And Kenji had read his memories closely enough to know how he behaved under fire and how he treated his men.

He lifted the desk phone and dialed from memory.

The line rang.

His hand lay flat on the desk beside the monitors, squared to their edge.

It rang a second time.

It rang a third.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Gunnery Sergeant Dominic Vance (50 to 59)*

### *Houston and the Gulf coast, 2052 to 2061*

The ledger was a green canvas-bound book from a surplus store, and its first page held no names. Vance had bought it the week after the treatment, out of a habit older than his stripes. It rode in the inside pocket of his coat, and he kept each entry to what he had walked himself.

**October 2052.** The phone rang on the porch well before dawn, where he had slept most nights since the treatment because a porch has a street in front of it. "Sergeant Vance, it's Kenji Himura," said a level voice. "A crew came through the dock of my warehouse east of Houston tonight. The sentries stopped them, but only after they were inside."

"Where does the road run?" Vance asked. "Not the one on the map. The one they came in by, because that's the one nobody was watching."

"I don't know. That's why I called you."

"Then I'll walk the ground," Vance said, and he drove out before light.

He walked the gravel lot from the fence to the dock and found what the machines could not see: a culvert where a car could sit unseen, and a rise across the road where a man with glasses could watch the dock all day. The sentries had held the door well. Nobody had been holding the road. He wrote that down, and then he wrote the first thing he needed, which was people who would stand where he told them.

**March 2053.** The first one he wanted, he already knew. Ashby had been the radio technician in the squad, nineteen years old and the youngest man in it, and he had walked out of the same ambush Vance had. Vance had found him work afterward, and then the hospice had taken them out of each other's lives. Since then the kid had refused to sign against the officer who sent them in, and the army had taken his pension for it.

Vance found him in Duluth, in a back room over a bait shop, repairing radios. Ashby looked up and said "Gunny" quietly, like a man who had not expected to say it again. A mechanic's stethoscope hung around his neck, the earpieces taped where they had cracked. A transformer on the wall had started to hum, and he tilted his head toward it, went still, and pulled its plug.

"The work is machines that mustn't fail and can't be explained," Vance said. "I need someone who'll tell me when one's about to."

The kid turned the taped stethoscope over in his hands. "Can I write down a fault nobody wants to hear about?"

"That's the whole job. Write it down, and bring it to me first."

Teodor Ashby came off the real machine in the Baytown warehouse in April with his knee and his lungs mended and his stethoscope back around his neck. By the end of the week he had put it to every servo on all three sentries.

**June 2053.** Galveston opened on a Tuesday, and from the first morning a trained person stood at each door. There were no robots among them, because a public clinic could show the world nothing it could not have built itself, and the sentries stayed in the warehouse. A person can read a stranger and speak to a deputy, which a sentry cannot. Vance had spent April and May in the clinic's staff room teaching the people he had, and only one of them, Ashby, had ever been a soldier.

The others were a retired insurance analyst, a fixer who had smoothed trouble for men he disliked, and a locksmith who could not stop looking at every lock in the building. All of them owed their lives to the machine in the back, and he had taught them what he knew: where to stand, which way a door opened, what a man looked like before he did something.

By the end of July the doors had gone to operatives he had trained for them, and he had moved each of the other three to the work they did best. The analyst took the shell companies and the ledgers behind them, which he read as easily as Vance read ground. The fixer took the city offices and the vendors, so trouble that would have reached the doors reached his phone instead. The locksmith took the doors themselves, every lock and hinge and key in the building, and told Vance more about its weak points than any walk of his had. Ashby went back to the sentries in Baytown. Twice that summer he wrote up a servo that sounded wrong a week before it failed, and Vance read both notes standing in the warehouse.

He drew the shape of it on the inside cover of the green book: a man at the top, a branch for each of the three who ran the work, the sentries under Ashby, and the doors along the bottom. It was the first page in the book that did not need him standing in it to hold it up.

The sentries were a stopgap for him too. A man who hears a fault early is wasted on three machines, but they were the only machines there were. The car park was the open side, and the city owned the lamps, so Vance wrote it down and let it go. At night he drove out to the warehouse to watch the sentries drill. They moved the way his point man used to move, and he had not expected to see that again.

**February 2061.** A Primitive-model Med-Pod went missing from a clinic outside Tulsa in a single night. The doors had stayed locked, and the night guard's log showed nothing crossing the floor. Ashby read the alarm panel's record and put in writing that it had stayed silent in the wrong way. The unit had left on a transfer order with the regional manager's signature, and the manager swore he had signed nothing.

Vance followed the order to a shell company, then to another shell, and then to a desk that did not exist. The analyst, who kept the books on those shells, found each link in a day. "Guards and locked doors won't stop this," he told Kenji. "I can hold a door against a man, but this came across a table, on paper, and I can't hold a door against a signature."

"What do you need?" Kenji asked.

"I don't know yet. When I do, you'll hear it from me first."

**October 2061.** He needed someone who read paper the way he read ground, and his own people did not. The analyst could follow a trail but could not see what a document left out. The Tulsa paper had crossed three borders on its way to nowhere, so he had the network pull the staff files of every ministry that licensed a clinic, in every country the clinics stood in, and he read them for weeks.

One paper was better than the rest. It was an analysis of the Ankara tariff schedules, and it was the only one that said what the schedules left out. The author was Maya Brooks, an analyst in Marseille, and he had been looking for someone strong enough to find the hand behind the paper, but her file ended in the spring with a leave of absence and a hospital ward. She was dying.

He sat a while with the file open. Then he turned back to the first page of the green book, the one that had held no names, and wrote hers.

## *Act I, 1st Movement · A2 · Kenji Himura (33)*

### *Galveston, 2053*

The clinic's license lay face down in a drawer in the monitor room, under a box of spare keys, and the name printed on it was not his. Kenji had taken it off the wall the night before, because a license names an owner, and an owner is someone a stranger can find. On the center monitor a young woman sat in the waiting room with her dress blues folded on the chair beside her, staring at the bare paint where it had hung.

He had watched her for most of three days from a chair set with its back to the wall, the way Vance had taught him to sit. She never wrote anything down, which was worse than if she had. She looked at the wall, then the front desk, then the chests of the staff one after another, and she kept returning to the places where something ought to have been. He had built a silence and trained a staff to keep it, and she was reading that silence the way he would read a gauge.

Her father's file lay beside the keyboard: pancreas, advanced; foreman, thirty years on hulls. Kenji had put the man in the hidden wing and not the public line, which bent the rule on the sheet pinned above his bench. He had told himself the machine did more good run properly, but the truer reason sat under that, that a man who could run a yard could run a building. He caught himself having thought it, and turned the file face down.

At ten past six the frosted doors opened on the third monitor, and the old man walked out on his own feet. The gray was gone from his face. He asked for eggs, and his daughter crossed the room and took his wrist, two fingers on the pulse, and held on long after the count was done. Kenji found he had stopped breathing, and started again.

The woman in gray scrubs made the offer. The daughter heard it through, which most people did not, and then she said no. Her father said only that it was her call.

In the drawer, under the license, lay a stack of blank intake cards. A card was for passing a name along, and he could not pass his. He sat with the pen uncapped over the white until he understood that nothing, sent on purpose, said something of its own.

He capped the pen.

He took the card.

The side stairs. Two at a time.

A man of Vance's stood at the side door and let him through without a word.

Wet air. Gulf heat. He went around the building at a walk that was nearly a run.

The front glass was fogged. Two shapes behind it. The old man's hand reaching.

Kenji crouched. Slid the card under the door.

It whispered on the tile.

He did not wait.

Corner. Brick at his back.

Behind him, the door wrenched open.

Silence. A gull working the street.

He pressed his palm flat to the wall. His heart would not slow.

Ten days later the front desk sent word up that a man had come back without his daughter and was asking for whoever ran the place. The staff had given him no name, and he had refused to leave without one. Kenji could have sent him away, but a man who carried what the wing had put in his head would keep asking, and Kenji wanted, though he would not have said so, to see what that man looked like when he was well.

He met them in the workroom, the one room in the building that never needed tidying. Tom Shepard stood by the door with his cap in both hands, and beside him stood a small woman with a steady look, who spoke before anyone else could.

"Who are you? Nobody in this building will say, and I'd like to hear it from the one person who can."

"Kenji," he said. "I answer for this building, for what happens in it and what doesn't. That's everything I can tell you that's true, and I'm sorry it isn't more."

"It's honest," Ruth said. "I'll take honest first."

"I came to say thank you," Tom said. "I've said it to a dozen people in scrubs who looked at me like I was reading them a bill, and I wanted to say it to the man it belongs to. So thank you, son. For my life, and for what you put in my head besides."

The word reached him a half second late. No one had called him that in this body, and no one had in the old life. His shoulders came up, and his hand found the pen on the bench and squared it against the clipboard.

"What do you need?" he said. "If the upload is giving you trouble, headaches or sleep or things arriving in the wrong order, I can have someone look at it today."

"Nothing's wrong, and I keep waiting for the bill." Tom turned the cap in his hands. "My daughter wouldn't come. She turned down your upload, and she's decided a place with nothing on the wall has something to hide. She's right, isn't she?"

"Yes. She's right to look."

Tom laughed, short and surprised. His eyes had drifted past Kenji to the bench along the wall, the vise, the half-built jig. "That vise is set for a left-handed man, and the bench is an inch too low for anyone. You've got a fine machine and nobody running the building around it, haven't you?"

"No one. I've been doing it between everything else, and I've been doing it badly."

"Then I could run it," Tom said, the way a man gives a measurement.

The pen sat square against the clipboard. Kenji picked it up, laid it down crooked, and left it there.
